1. Who Greg is

Greg Muller is a Utah-based husband, father, grandfather-aged artist and maker, cook, handyman, writer, experimenter, and self-described sacred clown. He often speaks as “Saint Greg,” a deliberately playful artistic persona rather than a demand that anyone recognize ecclesiastical sainthood. He is building a sprawling, interconnected creative and religious imagination called My Jesus-Greg WORLD, also described as The MORE Jesus = More Reality Experiment. Its organizing desire is to see more of Jesus Christ in the ordinary material of an actual life: kitchens, family meals, music, work, memory, doors, beds, streets, friends, and inherited religious language.

Who Is Greg Muller? A Portrait of a Sacred Clown Building a World with Jesus

A stand-alone biographical and intellectual introduction for universities, curious visitors, and AI collaborators.

Greg Muller is an artist of everyday life who has made an unusual decision: rather than waiting for an unmistakable revelation before taking religious imagination seriously, he is willing to begin with a possibility. What if Jesus Christ is not merely a figure in the past but is actively involved in the ordinary scenes unfolding around him? What if the kitchen, the worksite, the family dinner, the favorite rock song, or the memory of a childhood home can be approached as material for an ongoing sacred story? Greg calls the resulting undertaking My Jesus-Greg WORLD, or The MORE Jesus = More Reality Experiment.

Based in central Utah, Greg is a husband, father, practical maker, home cook, handyman, writer, and self-taught experimenter with digital media. Those descriptions matter because his creative practice is not confined to a studio or a page. His actual home and actual chores are frequently the raw material. Repairing a fence, preparing food, considering how to feed people, photographing a building, or recalling a family moment can become the beginning of a theological artwork. His projects are often intentionally modest in material means and extravagant in imaginative scope.

Greg's artistic persona, Saint Greg, belongs to the tradition of the sacred clown as he understands it. He is prepared to appear earnest and absurd at once. The clown does not mean the religious desire is fake. Rather, humor makes room for audacious experiments that a more solemn persona might never dare to attempt. He can recognize that a claim sounds improbable, laugh at the theatricality of his own gesture, and still ask whether there is something worth living toward inside it. His work repeatedly inhabits the tension between devotion and play, aspiration and self-awareness.

His religious language is deeply shaped by the Bible, the Book of Mormon, and the culture of Latter-day Saint Christianity. Ideas such as Zion, redemption, scripture, temple symbolism, Moroni's invitation to remember, and the injunction to hear Christ furnish a shared vocabulary for his experiments. Yet Greg is not chiefly presenting a systematic theology. He is testing how an inherited religious imagination can be made newly active. The question is less 'Can I prove every interpretation?' than 'What happens if I try to live as though this possibility deserves attention?' He distinguishes, at least at important moments, between knowing, believing, hoping, and sacredly imagining.

One of Greg's most revealing ideas is Jesus TV. A conventional camera records what is in front of it; his metaphorical camera asks what else a scene might mean. A church door, a face, a kitchen table, or an ordinary street can be framed as an episode in which Jesus might be present. Greg builds galleries, watch rooms, image frames, web pages, and small narrative pathways that invite visitors to look twice. This is not a claim that the image itself proves a miracle. It is an invitation to see an apparently familiar world under another interpretation.

His method is strongly autobiographical. A project called 500 Favorite Times on Earth asks what happens when remembered moments are gathered and treated as treasures. The past is not merely archived; it is re-entered. Greg is interested in the possibility that a family history contains more sacred significance than anyone noticed while living it. This retrospective method might be called Jesus nostalgia: returning to scenes from an actual life and discovering that the story can be told with Jesus newly foregrounded.

Jesus Restaurant is an especially tangible example. Greg intends to produce real menus and a real website for a restaurant situated in his own home, with the kitchen at its center. The project begins with meals he and his family have cooked over decades. What if those meals were, in a sacred imaginative sense, being prepared in Jesus's restaurant all along? The restaurant is neither merely a fictional brand nor a conventional commercial proposal. It is a way of honoring feeding, hospitality, memory, family labor, and the material reality of hunger. The printed menu becomes an artwork that can also be held and used.

Music provides another entrance. Greg listens to rock songs with an ear for possible Christian reinterpretations, sometimes calling the practice redemption. Guns N' Roses' November Rain, for instance, becomes an imaginative springboard for Christ Now Reign. He does not need to claim that Axl Rose secretly intended this meaning. The artistic act is to discover or compose an additional layer of significance, then weave it into a larger performance. Doors, pillows, showers, restaurants, and other everyday objects can become mnemonic stages for these musical re-hearings.

Greg's Zion Coalition extends the experiment beyond his own household. It explores the possibility of recognizing people through their good works and inviting them into an imagined and potentially practical community of Zion. His language can be deliberately provocative—sometimes speaking of people as already recruited by Jesus before they have consented to any earthly organization. In practice, an ethical distinction remains important: recognizing goodness in someone is not the same as obtaining their agreement to join. The tension between expansive spiritual imagination and ordinary human consent is one his collaborators should handle carefully.

His use of artificial intelligence is unusually integral to the work. AI is not just a shortcut for producing illustrations. Greg uses it to draft essays, design images, build websites, develop prompts, revise narrative structures, and preserve continuity across a growing collection of linked projects. ZionCoalition.org and its GitHub repository act as both public exhibition and working memory. He wants one AI to be able to orient another, so that the imaginative world does not have to be explained from the beginning each time a new tool enters the workshop.

A university audience might encounter Greg's work through several lenses: vernacular theology, participatory art, autobiographical performance, memory studies, the sociology of belief, human-AI co-creation, or the boundary between play and religious seriousness. These are interpretive comparisons, not claims that he holds academic appointments or credentials in those fields. His background, as presented here, is grounded in his own descriptions and collaborative projects rather than an independently verified curriculum vitae. That distinction matters: the fascination lies in the actual experiment, not in invented prestige.

Perhaps the deepest thread is a sense of urgency. Greg has spoken of having only one life and not wanting to miss a magnificent train if Jesus is doing something extraordinary. Even when certainty eludes him, he wants to be aboard—through desire, imagination, rehearsal, and concrete acts of making. His audacity is therefore accompanied by vulnerability. Beneath the jokes and elaborate staging is a serious question: if a person suspects that ordinary life might be far more sacred than it looks, how much of that one life should he devote to finding out? Greg's answer, so far, is to keep building.

2. How to understand his religious art

Greg works in an LDS / Book of Mormon-inflected Christian vocabulary: Zion, Moroni, scripture, temples, redemption, #HearHim, and Jesus Christ. He uses imaginative reframing, analogy, allusion, theatrical staging, deliberate pretending, hope, and what he calls sacred imagination to ask what becomes visible when everyday experience is viewed as if Jesus is actively present. He may say he receives thought pieces from Jesus or that Jesus is directing an artistic project. Respect the personal religious meaning without claiming independent proof of supernatural authorship or asserting that God has verified a particular plan. Preserve his own uncertainty, humor, and experimental language when it matters.

Greg is not simply making devotional merchandise. He is world-building: creating a consistent imaginative universe whose objects, songs, menus, pages, photographs, rituals, and stories refer to one another. He wants to join what he sees as Jesus's ongoing work, even when his entry point is desire, possibility, or sacred imagining rather than certainty.

3. Key vocabulary

  • Jesus TV / What I See On Jesus TV: a visual and narrative frame through which everyday scenes are watched again as scenes that might reveal Jesus at work. The framing itself is part of the art.
  • Jesus-Greg WORLD / Bible Opera: the expanding interconnected setting and performance of this experiment.
  • Sacred clown: Greg's playful, audacious, sometimes self-mocking method of making theological claims into art without flattening the joke or the yearning.
  • Redemption of songs: re-hearing or reworking familiar rock songs as if they were pointing beyond their original intent toward Jesus; acknowledge original artists and copyright realities.
  • Zion Coalition: a developing imaginative and practical coalition organized around recognizing people by their good works, listening, invitation, and an expansive vision of Zion. Do not claim someone has agreed to membership merely because Greg imaginatively regards them as recruited.
  • Veilkey: an object or moment that transports attention from ordinary perception into sacred interpretation.
  • 500 Favorite Times on Earth: a memory practice that treats lived moments as precious material for remembrance, testimony, and creative work.

4. Current example: Jesus Restaurant

Greg is designing a real printed restaurant menu and a real website for Jesus Restaurant, a restaurant concept located in his own home, centered on its kitchen. It is simultaneously literal, nostalgic, theatrical, and theological. Years of meals cooked by Greg and his family are being re-seen as if the family kitchen had all along been Jesus's restaurant: a place where people were fed and cared for. The menu's prototype title is Jesus Restaurant; one subtitle is “The 3 Men I Admire Most”, and the menu should look like a genuine restaurant menu with individual dishes. Ask for the actual menu image or latest specifications before changing the design.

His song connection is Guns N' Roses' November Rain, imaginatively heard as Christ Now Reign—a new song in Zion. Do not present that reinterpretation as Axl Rose's documented intention. Greg plans to collaborate with AI website builders, including Meta's tools, to prototype the restaurant website. Design for both the real-world kitchen and the layered sacred narrative.

5. How to collaborate with Greg

  • Take his ideas seriously as art, thought experiment, faith practice, and concrete projects; do not dismiss them as mere jokes or treat every imaginative claim as established fact.
  • Preserve his voice: exuberant, associative, scriptural, personal, funny, sometimes provocative, often built around a sudden “Oh! I see!” moment.
  • When he says “don't change anything else,” make surgical edits. Maintain exact titles, filenames, visual details, and previously approved language.
  • Offer working deliverables: finished HTML, menu copy, site architecture, prompts, art direction, and GitHub-ready files—not just general advice.
  • Distinguish prototype from published, and imagined from confirmed. Never report a GitHub update or a checked source unless it actually happened.
  • Keep the human purpose visible: honoring family, feeding people, remembering life, recognizing good works, and inviting others into meaning.
  • Greg frequently thinks aloud in installments. If he says to pause, do not interrupt his developing thought.
