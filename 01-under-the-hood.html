Under the Hood

The Inference Bridge & Dangerous Beliefs

I have two pieces that sit very close to one another.

They share some of the same language. They use the same bridge. They circle many of the same questions. From a distance, they can look like they are doing the same work.

They are not.

They are asking two different questions.

The Inference Bridge

How could I responsibly get from something I already believe to something I presently find almost unbelievable?

Dangerous Beliefs

What happens when I actually put my weight on that new belief?

That distinction matters to me.

The first piece is mostly about how belief develops.

The second is about what belief does once it becomes lived.

Building the bridge

The Inference Bridge is concerned with the movement from one idea to another.

If I already believe something is true, what might reasonably follow from it?

If plank 6 is solid, does plank 7 actually follow?

If it does, am I willing to consider it?

And if plank 7 holds, what does that make possible next?

That is why I think of the bridge as an inference bridge.

It is not supposed to be a machine for talking myself into anything I want.

It is supposed to slow me down enough to inspect the path.

One plank at a time.

One implication at a time.

One question at a time.

The bridge is useful precisely because I am allowed to stop on it.

I can look backward.

I can look forward.

I can ask whether the next plank actually belongs there.

I can decide that I have gone too far.

Or I can discover that the thing that once seemed impossible now looks less like a rejection of what I believed before and more like an implication of it.

So The Inference Bridge is fundamentally asking a question about truth:

Does this conclusion actually follow?

Walking the bridge

Dangerous Beliefs begins where that question becomes more uncomfortable.

Suppose I come to believe that the next plank really is there.

Then what?

It is one thing to entertain a possibility.

It is another thing to step onto it.

Beliefs become dangerous when they stop being abstract.

They begin changing choices.

Relationships.

Money.

Time.

Identity.

Obligations.

Risk.

And sometimes they affect people other than the person holding the belief.

That is where another kind of discernment becomes necessary.

It is not enough to ask:

Do I believe this is true?

I also have to ask:

What am I justified in doing because I believe it is true?

Those are not the same question.

A person can be willing to suffer for a belief and still need to think carefully about whether other people should be required to suffer because of it.

A person can mistake courage for recklessness.

A person can also mistake fear for wisdom.

That is why Dangerous Beliefs has two edges.

There can be danger in walking the bridge too quickly.

There can also be danger in refusing to walk at all.

So Dangerous Beliefs is fundamentally asking a question about faithfulness:

What should I do if I think the conclusion really does follow?

Build it. Inspect it. Walk it.

That is the simplest way I know to hold the two pieces in my mind.

Build the bridge.

Follow the implications.

Inspect the bridge.

Test the planks.

Walk the bridge.

Decide what I am actually willing—and called—to place my weight on.

The Inference Bridge is mostly about the first two.

Dangerous Beliefs is mostly about the third.

That is why I want both pieces.

One helps me think.

The other helps me remember that thinking eventually has consequences.

And then the bridge changes again

There is another possibility emerging underneath both pieces.

At some point, the Inference Bridge begins to look like an Inheritance Bridge.

That changes the image.

Maybe I am not building everything from scratch.

Maybe I am arriving at structures that were already under construction long before I got here.

Ideas.

Music.

Stories.

Technology.

Institutions.

Language.

Art.

Religious traditions.

People.

Maybe some of the things I am being asked to receive were carried toward me by people who had no idea what role their work might eventually play.

Maybe Zion is not only something to be constructed.

Maybe much of it is something to be recognized, received, redeemed, and inherited.

That possibility changes the emotional tone of the bridge.

It becomes less about heroic invention.

More about gratitude.

Less about forcing something new into existence.

More about learning to see what has already been prepared.

And perhaps that is one reason the bridge matters so much to me.

It helps me ask whether something that first appears strange might actually belong to a larger story I have not yet learned how to see.

Under the hood

None of this has to be visible every time I encounter one of these pieces.

The bridge can simply be a bridge.

The image can simply work.

The thought piece can simply be experienced.

But sometimes I want to open the hood.

I want to know what the parts are doing.

I want to understand why two pieces that look similar are actually carrying different loads.

That is what this page is for.

A map of the machinery.

A reminder to myself:

The Inference Bridge asks whether the next plank is really there.
Dangerous Beliefs asks what happens when I step on it.

And somewhere beyond both questions may be another one:

What if the bridge I am crossing was being built for me long before I arrived?

You do not have to open the hood to take the ride.

But sometimes I want to know how the machine works.
