Welcome to the Podium Museum

Today I am founding another museum in My Jesus-Greg WORLD. I am calling it, provisionally, the Podium Museum. It is not yet finished. It is a growing collection of stories, people, songs, practices, thought pieces, and sacred associations. I will continue bringing its exhibits together, and links to them will accumulate here.

The focal point is a familiar sight: the microphone at the speakers' stand in my church. Much of what happens at that stand gathers around the microphone. Words are spoken, amplified, heard. The sacrament and remembrance of Jesus are more central to worship than any microphone, of course. Yet the microphone remains a powerful visual symbol of the formal speaking role.

And now I have a curious way of traveling there in my imagination: a carrot.

The VeilKey

In my Jesus-Greg WORLD, ordinary objects can become VeilKeys (Portkeys): reminders that carry my attention into meaningful scenes. The Jesus Pillow and the shower have become such keys. Now comes the Jesus Carrot.

I used to eat regular carrots. From now forward, in the language of my personal practice, I eat Jesus Carrots. I believe this humble object may have been waiting, from the foundations of my world, to play this symbolic role.

The carrot resembles a handheld microphone. In my imagination it is one end of a child's tin-can telephone. At the other end is the actual microphone I see in sacrament meeting each week. The association works both ways: at home the carrot recalls the church microphone; at church the microphone recalls the carrot.

The Museum's Opening Song

First foray · Tune in

What is the sound?

When a VeilKey takes me somewhere, the first question is: What is the sound? The Jesus Carrot has a song, and that song is the emotional doorway into this museum. Its yearning explains the carrot-microphone: a lifelong longing to share news from Jesus, the sadness of not being heard, and the freedom of sharing without demanding an audience.

Enter Sound Exhibit 01: “The Things God Shares with Me” — redeemed lyrics, musical reference, and interpretation →

Every VeilKey has an associated song that introduces its theme. The Jesus Carrot's song is “The Things God Shares with Me,” my redeemed adaptation of Bob Dylan's “Make You Feel My Love,” also famously sung by Adele and others.

The original song's yearning becomes my longing to share what I understand as news from Jesus. Yet the song also confesses a difficult truth: I cannot make other people want what I want to share. Its final movement turns outward: God may have things to share directly with them, too.

Exhibit One: The Microphone I No Longer Need

Since childhood I have been rambunctious: a sacred clown, an explorer, a disruptor, sometimes for good and sometimes not. My mind discovers a frontier and my mouth wants to take everyone there. I have often hijacked conversations, mistaken the invitation to participate for permission to redirect the entire gathering, and failed to notice when others did not want to follow.

I used to speak at church in ways that could be provocative, surprising, or simply too much for the occasion. Some people appreciated it. Others understandably did not. My wife gently—and sometimes plainly—encouraged restraint for years.

Eventually I came to understand my restraint as a spiritual invitation. I describe it as finally hearing from Jesus what others had been trying to tell me. Over several years I largely stopped volunteering comments and testimonies in church. Not because I had nothing to say, but because having something to say does not oblige other people to hear it.

There was sacrifice and sadness in that surrender. The redeemed Dylan song carries some of that melancholy. But it also carries the possibility of rest. The rocket engine remains; it need not launch every time someone comes within earshot.

I have not stopped speaking. I am learning to stop requiring the room to become my audience.

I think of Ether 12:27: weak things can become strong through humility and grace. I don't need my curiosity, intensity, or creative energy erased. I want them redeemed—redirected into songs, artwork, writing, invitations, and spaces that people may freely choose to enter.

Exhibit Two: The Formal Microphone and the Informal Carrot

🎤 The formal microphone

The talking stick: scheduled talks, teachers, offices, institutional roles, recognized authorities, and the people entrusted with a particular room's attention.

🥕 The informal carrot

My unconventional calling: playful, experimental, voluntary, and free of any requirement that someone become a captive audience.

I have often envied those who hold the microphone: general authorities, prophets, professors, teachers. I once wanted to be a college professor myself. I joke about wanting a captive audience; I even taught sociology in a women's prison in Huntsville, Texas, where the phrase had a literal edge.

At church, the microphone reminds me that the formal speaking roles belong to those who have been asked to fill them. I can respect that structure without demanding a turn simply because I have listened to someone else for hours.

Here is the paradox: I voluntarily return every week to the place where I practice not needing the microphone. For a free spirit, that is a meaningful form of surrender. In my own reading of discipleship, it recalls “not my will, but thine” and the invitation to take up one's cross.

Exhibit Three: The INNkeepers and the Manger

For years I have written about the INNkeepers—the INN crowd, those who are INN vogue, INN dispensable, INN portant, INN control, and INN fluencers. They have the roles, resources, authority, polish, and access that make institutions work. My writing sometimes has an edge, but I want this qualification unmistakable: Jesus loves the INNkeepers. And I am learning to love and appreciate them, too.

In my 2015 Born Again story, I understood myself as being called away from knocking on the cool people's door and toward the manger scene. Not because the INN is evil, but because I need not be admitted to it to do the work I believe is mine.

That is why the formal microphone is such a useful reminder. I am not called to seize it. I am called to create in other places—and to honor those who serve in the formal ones.

Exhibit Four: Speaking Without Capturing an Audience

The sculptor who continues to make his sculpture without demanding universal approval, and the little business cards offered in Manti without demanding a response, belong in this room. Both remind me that the work can be made and the invitation extended without commandeering the attention of others.

That is a more restful way to live: make the work, put it where people may find it, and let them decide whether to enter.

The Practice: A Carrot, a Microphone, a Reminder

When I eat a carrot, I may let it become my little pretend microphone. I remember the real microphone at church. I remember my urge to speak, the mistakes I have made, the people who have listened, and the people who would rather not. I remember the sadness in the song—and its liberating ending.

When I see the church microphone, I can remember my Jesus Carrot. I can sit in the congregation, receive what is offered, honor the people speaking, and allow my own work to await another time and another forum.

I can honor the formal microphone without needing to possess it. I can speak through other means, without requiring anyone to listen.

The Museum Is Open—and Under Construction

This page establishes the room, not the final arrangement of its exhibits. More people, stories, objects, songs, and thought pieces will be brought here. As the collection grows, the museum will acquire new doors and links. Its centerpiece remains the microphone, and the carrot remains the VeilKey that takes me there.

There is a Jesus Pillow. There is Jesus bread. And now, for me, there is a Jesus microphone—approached by way of a carrot.
