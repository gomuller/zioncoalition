THE SINGULARITY OF THOUGHT

Intelligence · Attention · Awareness · Reality

A conceptual companion to Jesus, the Very Thought of Thee

The MORE Jesus = More Reality Experiment — Saint Greg

What if the future of intelligence and the future of attention have something surprising to say to each other? And what if the most important transformation is not the number of thoughts we can generate, but the way a single enduring orientation changes the meaning of all the others?

1. The technological singularity

The technological singularity is a speculative idea: sufficiently advanced artificial intelligence might improve intelligence so rapidly that the resulting changes become difficult for us to predict. It is about accelerating capability and potentially cascading transformations.

It is not an established future event. But as a thought experiment it raises a profound question: what happens when a process begins amplifying its own power?

2. A singularity of thought?

Now imagine a different kind of convergence—not of machines, but of attention. A human mind ordinarily moves among thousands of concerns. What happens when those concerns are increasingly organized around one enduring relationship, question, value, or purpose?

I call this a singularity of thought. I am proposing a metaphor, not announcing a recognized scientific phenomenon. The point is not that every thought becomes identical. It is that many different thoughts may acquire a common orientation.

One thought does not replace all the others. It becomes a place to which the others can return.

This is potentially expansive rather than restrictive. The mind might discover more connections, not fewer, because it has developed a recurring lens through which to notice them.

3. What Jon Kabat-Zinn taught at Google

In his Google talks on mindfulness, Jon Kabat-Zinn presented a practical discipline of awareness: pay attention deliberately to the present moment; notice when the mind wanders; and return without condemning yourself for wandering.

In mindfulness practice, the breath can serve as an anchor. But the essential lesson is not that the breath is the only worthy object of attention. It is that the quality of attending matters, and that returning is itself part of the practice.

There is an important qualification: Kabat-Zinn's mindfulness is generally oriented toward open, nonjudgmental awareness, not permanent fixation on a single doctrine or idea. A devotional adaptation is an additional choice, not something his teaching necessarily prescribes.

4. The thought and the practice of thinking

There are at least two variables here: what I repeatedly attend to and how I attend to it. One concerns the object of attention; the other concerns the cultivated capacity to notice, wander, and return.

Repeated practices can influence habits, interpretations, and responses. That does not prove that a particular chosen object changes the brain in a specific way, nor does it settle the spiritual interpretation of an experience.

Still, it invites an experiment: choose a recurring anchor, practice returning, and observe how your perception and conduct develop over time.

5. An attention explosion?

If technological singularity imagines an intelligence explosion, perhaps we can imaginatively ask about an attention explosion: a growing capacity to notice meaning, relationships, possibilities, and previously overlooked dimensions of ordinary experience.

This is analogy, not neuroscience. More concentrated attention does not automatically mean more truth; it can also produce blind spots. A sound experiment must remain open to correction, evidence, and other people's perspectives.

But there is a beautiful possibility here: one sustained concern might illuminate a thousand seemingly unrelated experiences.

6. My particular wager: Jesus

For my own experiment, I choose Jesus as the recurring orientation. I begin the day with a planned first thought: Jesus, the very thought of thee.

I guess. I believe. I sacredly imagine. I wager. I am not claiming that the singularity metaphor proves divine causation. I am describing a deliberately practiced way of living and watching what follows.

The breath, the pillow, the music, a conversation, an ordinary task: each may become another occasion to remember. The aim is not to think about nothing but Jesus, but to find more opportunities to relate the rest of life to Him.

What if the singularity is not the moment when I finally think about only one thing, but the moment when one enduring relationship begins to illuminate everything else?

That question leads directly into the opening scene of my daily opera: Jesus, the Very Thought of Thee — The First Thought, the Jesus Pillow, and the Singularity of Thought.
