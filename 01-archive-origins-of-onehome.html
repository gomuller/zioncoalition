Family First…..From Father To Son, To Son 
I am keeping my[a] own children in mind as I write this book.  I am imagining them in future, when they have their own family with young children.  Their lives are happy, but they see a need to improve their homes. They call us and ask for help. Their lives and circumstances are different than ours were.  They have different spouses, children, income, tastes, skills, knowledge, abilities and needs.  Nevertheless, they have many of the same needs and desires that we had as we were raising them.  They want their food to be at least as tasty, nutritious, safe, and easy to prepare as ours was.  They also remember there were some things about the routines, practices and traditions that were part of how we planned, gathered, stored, prepared, ate and cleaned up food in our home.  


To help them best we realize that we need to do more than just give them a collection of our recipes and some cooking tips.  After much thought we decide that we should teach our children everything about the food part of our home.  We make it our mission to write a book to teach our children about our home, our “OneHome”.  We determine that we will teach our children about:


* ALL WE HAVE in terms of food plans, traditions, routines, practices, and so forth.  We will put in writing anything and everything having to do with the food part of our home.  We will call the food part of our home our food system.


* WHY WE HAVE what we do.  We will provide written justification for everything we do.  Our children will understand why we did things a certain way and not another way.  We will describe the principles, our wants, our needs and the rules-of-thumb we followed as we built our food system.  They will become aware of the circumstances in our lives that in some instances dictated and in other instances  allowed certain choices to be made with regard to food.    


* HOW TO COPY & CUSTOMIZE.  We will provide step-by-step instructions on how to copy all or part of our food system.  It is unlikely that our entire food system would be a perfect fit for my children’s homes, let alone other people’s homes.   However, our home is enough alike that my children and others can copy desired parts of the food system for their own needs.  Our food system will be useful to them even if they don’t copy it.  It can act as a template or framework they can refer to as they build a food system for their home.  Because we completely describe and justify every part of our food system our children can understand what are all the parts are that make up the food system.  They can then determine how they would like to build those parts in their own homes.


HOW TO TEACH their own children and other people.  Our children are wisely looking forward to the time when their own children will be establishing their own homes.  Our book can act as a pattern for our children to follow, showing them how to “reveal” their own food system to their children and others to build upon and improve.

OneHome is a plan to help people improve their home.  As the name suggests, it is a plan based on one home.  The Muller home.  Everything having to do with our home is part of the OneHome plan.  Any activity that takes place in our home is included.  How we do laundry is the OneHome plan for doing laundry.  Our menu is the OneHome menu plan.  Our financial strategies are the OneHome strategies for finance.  Our religious practices are the OneHome religious practices.  Our daily routines are the routines of OneHome.  


The OneHome plan is [will be] presented as a virtual home on the Internet.  A visitor to OneHome will see a house from a distance, with a car in the driveway.  Zooming in, the visitor can tap on the front door to seek entry.  Inside or outside the visitor can click on any part of the home to learn about what is there, why it is there, and a step-by-step set of instructions of how to put it in their own home.  


OneHome is, therefore, an example home, a model home.  OneHome is a model in four different ways:  First, for the family who's home it is OneHome serves as a tangible way to determine what they have, want, and need in their home. Second, OneHome is built so that other families or individuals can easily copy desired parts of OneHome and adapt them to their own home needs. Third, OneHome is a model that can be followed by people who desire to develop their own home as a model for others.  Fourth, OneHome is an integrative framework that other programs can be built upon.  In this article I discuss each of these uses of OneHome in greater detail.




ONEHOME AS A MODEL FOR SELF & OTHERS
A key strength of OneHome is that it makes all parts of the home visible and manipulatable.  OneHome reveals that which is usually implicit in a home.  By completely describing OneHome, what is often to the home owner obvious (e.g. the way shopping is done, the way finances are handled) becomes inescapable to the home viewer.  


OneHome not only describes what is in the home, but explains why it is there.  The desired principles and attendant circumstances that guided the selection of a given item, practice, tradition, or routine are spelled out.  In this way OneHome is unique.  While everyone has reasons why their home is like it is, few (if any) people ever put their reasons in writing.  Reasons or justifications for every single item, practice, and routine in our home will be included in OneHome.  


In the virtual laundry room, for example, if a visitor clicks on the bottle of spot remover they will read (or listen to) an explanation about why GooGone® is currently designated as the official OneHome spot remover.  They will learn that only a highly effective, if expensive, spot cleaner is sufficient for the current needs of our family.  They will learn that we have need for a highly effective spot cleaner, as we do not have lots of money to buy replacement clothes for our four young children.  They will be alerted to the fact that our family recreation includes painting and other stain-producing activities.  They will learn that our parenting style allows for much outdoor play and spot-getting.  They will be apprised of every other relevant and significant reason we have for choosing the particular product.  If effectiveness, for instance, was the primary justification for designating, say GooGone®, as the official OneHome spot remover, then links will be provided to document the logic used to conclude GooGone.  Such links may lead a visitor to a bibliography of books, articles in trade magazines, a compilation of newsgroup articles that were read or personal experiences that were had, or people that were consulted to come to our OneHome selection of spot remover.  








This comprehensive and extensive "revealing" of the home allows the OneHome family to assess their home globally in great detail.  In this way OneHome is a good planning device.  Changes to one part of the home can be considered in light of the family principles, values, and circumstances, as well as in light of how changes will affect other parts of the home.  Changes can be considered and recommend in a way that is wise.  In a way that is more sensitive to the resources that are available to the home.  In a way that is more appropriately aligned with the desired principles and values of those who live in the home.  In a way that more accurately targets the needs of the home.  And finally, in a way that can be more trusted by the gatekeeper (owners) of the home.  


Having the home "revealed" also allows others to make informed suggestions. By clearly describing and providing justifications for every part of OneHome, the home and all its processes is opened not only to being copied, but critiqued and modified.  A strong value underlying OneHome is the value of change.  Not haphazard change.  Not change for its own sake.  But change born of necessity, change born of insufficiency, change born of the weight of good reason and the promise of home improvement.  


OneHome is a plan that values reason-based critical assessments and has as its start the belief that there are better ways to live life.  One of the primary purposes that extreme measures and great pains are taken to describe and justify what OneHome looks like is the faith that by doing so we can be led to better practices in the future.  Improvement of home-life must start by first taking assessment of what we have (e.g. values, life practices, unique circumstances), before we consider what we want and need.  Therefore, OneHome seeks to make the obvious inescapable.  By revealing the reason why a given feature of OneHome is like it is, that reasoning can be subject to constructive critique.  By first coming to an understanding of why something is like it is we can make more informed judgements about whether and how we might change it.  OneHome expresses the value of reasoned debate.  Every aspect of OneHome is incorporated and developed based on clearly expressed and identifiable reasoning.  This reasoning gives OneHome practices a tentative, though determined permanency.  


OneHome is a principle focused home.  OneHome is built upon three main principles: order, simplicity, and sufficiency.   While other principles could be paramount-- some homes emphasize environmental friendliness, some frugality, some recreation, some safety--these three have been selected.  Because OneHome emphasizes order, simplicity and sufficiency does not mean it is not frugal, fun or safe.  It simply means that in OneHome an especially strong effort is made to make all parts of the home orderly, simple, and sufficient for the needs of the OneHome family.  It means in OneHome an orderly, simple and sufficient way of doing something in the home will generally always triumph (be selected) over other desirable ways of doing things.


The identification and selection of principles that guide OneHome is not a small matter.  As suggested above, visitors to OneHome will see every aspect of OneHome.  There are thousands of things in the home they view, thousands of routines and traditions…all representing thousands upon thousands of decisions made by OneHome.  To understand why this and not that is done in OneHome, to understand the underlying logic behind the home, to be able to ask wise questions and provide good advice, the visitor needs to understand what the main guiding principles of OneHome are.


OneHome seeks to maximize specific principles.  Order, simplicity and sufficiency are the main principles being maximized in OneHome.  Nevertheless, each part of OneHome may require attending to and maximizing other relevant principles.  For instance, the OneHome OneWeek Menu System seeks to maximize five main principles, 1. taste, 2. nutrition density, 3. ease of preparation, 4. cost, and 5. correlation of recipe ingredients.  While these principles guide recipe selection and other decisions, they collectively represent an orderly, simple and sufficient way to evaluate meals in OneHome.  Hence,  order, simplicity and sufficiency in OneHome often operate as overarching principles.


OneHome is, therefore, a conclusion about what is sufficient in terms of order, simplicity and sufficiency in a home.  Like a well reasoned conclusion drawn in collegial argument, OneHome stands firm as the way a home should be, but is simultaneously open to modification if good reasons for change can be brought forward.  


OneHome also provides a step-by-step set of instructions for how people can acquire the items and implement the practices, traditions, and routines of OneHome.  


Universal principles
As suggested the principles of order, simplicity and sufficiency are not the only principles that operate throughout OneHome.  A significant feature of OneHome, a feature that makes OneHome orderly and simple, is that there are global or universal principles that extend in and out of every aspect of OneHome.  The same logic (or principles) that guide placement of things in the kitchen are the same principles that guide placement of things in the bathroom, the bedroom, the yard, the medicine cabinet, etc.  The same principles used to manage the OneHome wardrobe are the same that manage food, non-perishables, and all other tangibles in the home.


OneHome value of order, simplicity and sufficiency express an aspiration to discover principles within the home that can be globally or universally applied throughout the home.  It is orderly, it is simple and it is (my) definition of sufficient to have a home that all the answers are the same, at least in principle.  








Even though OneHome asserts a particular way to live, an accommodation is made.  Alternatives are catalogued.  Alternatives for the OneHome family (in case we get more poor [blow nose in clothes] or more rich [blow nose in puffs], that allow quick changes. Evaluative snapshots of OneHome will be taken at different points along its development.  This will not only allow visitors to see the possibility for home improvement (we were once there now we are here), but will serve as a type of multiple models (once we used paper and pencil to plan menus--that works and is sufficient…but now we can maximize ease and we use this program)  Alternatives are also offered as links to other home models, homes based on other desired principles and values than those of OneHome…homes that may more closely align with certain visitors to OneHome.


ONEHOME A MODEL FOR OTHER HOME MODELS
OneHome…again and again
OneHome lends itself as an example of how people can develop their own home as a model that can be used by others seeking home improvement.  In other words, OneHome is meant to have babies…to be a foundation for replication.  The processes and structure underlying OneHome can act as a model for building other models.  All of these alternative home models can then be linked to OneHome and visa versa.  This then establishes OneHome not only as a virtual model for visitors interested in home improvement, but as a portal for helping them travel to other homes that might better meet their needs.


One Size Fits…Some
Certainly, a few visitors to OneHome may proclaim, "Yes! OneHome is exactly what we were looking for; it will work for us and we want our home to be just like it!"  They then have the benefit of easily copying and adapting every single aspect of OneHome.  Our ways of cooking becomes their way of cooking; our style of parenting becomes their way of parenting, our recreation choices are theirs.  


Of course no family will find that OneHome meets all of their needs.   And some families will find that OneHome represents a far cry from what is possible or desirable for them.  The environment, circumstances, principles and values that gave rise to OneHome (i.e. the Muller home) are not the same that will guide the building of other homes.  Compared to the Muller family other families may have more or fewer children.  Their children may be older or younger.  They may have more or less social or financial means than the OneHome family.  The Muller family may care more or less about gardening or particular types of recreation than other families do.  And so on.


What is needed is the development of other models.  A model for every general status in life.  A model for the rich and retired.  A model for the young, childless college couple.  A model for the single, middle income professional.  A model for the middle class dual income earner family highly interested in outdoor education.  A model for people living with little margins--people in third-world or third-world like conditions.  And so on.


OneHome provides a universal and comprehensive framework that can be used by others in developing these models.   OneHome is only one home, but will give rise to hundreds, even thousands of different home models.  OneHome will be built as the base home, the place to start.   It will serve as a model for other similar homes--homes that will serve as base homes for other model homes.  The second home could be called, OneHome2 (or OH2), the next OneHome3 (OH3), and so on.


Model building will not be for everyone.  Some people will simply want to make improvements in their home without taking the extra step of documenting their developments in ways that are accessible and replicable by others.  However, I believe there are many people and organizations that would be interested in building model homes.  






OneHome Will Recruit-Consult-Advise
I will seek to find and assist people to build alternative OneHome-like models.  OneHome will show them how to become experts in their own home.  OneHome provides them a method and support that they can orderly, simply and sufficiently develop and disseminate their progress for others benefits.  As these people develop and become experts of their own homes they will act as home consultants giving direct assistance to families who have similar needs as their own.  


Can't lose
Recruiting people to be home model builders will be accomplished in part by pointing out the benefits that accrue to home model builders.   First, model builders get to further develop something they were likely already interested in improving--their own home.  Unlike many business ventures, being a model home builder is a no-fail proposition.  If the only people who are served by the improvements made in a home is the model builder's family, the building has not been in vain.  The hours and hours of planning, developing, and implementing are not wasted. 
What's good for the goose…
Second, because the model builders will fashion their model after OneHome, their model will be positioned to easily accommodate new ideas and developments that are gained by other models in the OneHome Sytem (all the OneHome-related home models, OneHome2, OneHome3, etc. etc.)   For example, if OneHome1 (the Muller family) finds a way to improve their, say TravelKit (a kit for short road trips), the Jones family (who has adapted a similar kit making practices into their home) can easily understand and make the change if they desire.  
Cornering the market…
Third, any OneHome model builder will have something that doesn't exist anywhere else.  Because the development of OneHome1, OneHome2, and OneHome3, and so on is intimately tied to the conditions of an actual home, there exists a huge range of different needs each will fill.  Thus, there is a potential that the home model that the person is building will become an important contact point for at least a segment of the population (the segment that with a family that most closely resembles the model builder's family).


ONEHOME AS AN INTEGRATIVE FRAMEWORK FOR OTHER PROGRAMS
OneHome acts as a integrative framework for developing other home-oriented programs.  For instance, the OneHome plan could serve as a design for implementing school reform.  Many educators recognize that school needs to do more to improve the home life of students and their families.  Over the last few years the federal government has spent hundreds of millions of dollars on School-to-Work initiatives trying to increase the strength of connection between that school and work.  This makes sense.  People spend lots of time in their lives in two environments--school and work.  It makes good sense to try and make both more relevant to one another.  But what is the missing piece here?  What other place, besides school and work do people spend most of their time in this life.  Of course, at home.  If School-to-Work is justified, School-to-Home and Home-to-Work are justified.  OneHome can provide a framework for integrating academic curriculum with needs of the home (e.g. compare career field areas with home system areas).  OneHome can provide a framework for integrating home more effectively with work.  


Other programs could benefit similarly.  OneHome could be a foundation for developing comprehensive reform and improvement in programs for independent living for the disabled and elderly.  OneHome could become a tool used by social service providers (e.g. welfare officers could direct clients to OneHome consultants for models of home improvement).  OneHome could be used to develop industry interventions in the home.  As telecommuting heats up, concern about how to order the home and home business will become a large issue (e.g. productivity, ergonomics, etc.).  OneHome can be a basis for addressing the connection between home and church and community. [I would like to see the LDS church sponsor some model homes--integrating religion, providing definitions of sufficiency to membership, etc.]


Another way that OneHome can be used is to tie all models of home together.  A person could look at the OneHome manner of peeling potatoes, find it not sufficient.  With a click they could be on a web page that has collated tens or hundreds of different ways people peel potatoes (or ways people use or dispose potato skin refuse), these ways could be ordered by main guiding principles (sufficiency, frugality, environmentalism) or home environments (young children, older children, elderly, disabled).  Each activity in the home could be addressed and collated.  OneHome would be the starting place that all other links could be accessed from.


OneHome needs to be viewed as a dynamic home, always representing an act of balance between change and constancy.  Perhaps each year it should be updated to reflect selected, major factors--- new information, new technology, change of economy, broader culture, change in children and adults life stages, job loss, health problems (e.g. a child can't eat spicy anymore)etc. etc.  Therefore an assessment tool(s) is required, one that can give immediate feedback.  


OneHome will have core elements that will not change much (small in-home variance and between-home variance).  For example, the Muller family pizza and video routine will likely continue.  It is anticipated that pizza will always cook at 450 degrees on a pizza stone at OneHome (this is how it is done by the best pizza makers).  However, the types of pizzas that will be made will increase.

A key idea within the HOMES approach is the notion that all home life can be thought of as a collection of such activities.  This leads to the HOMES’  “systems” perspective. A system is a group of related objects, things, or activities that form a unified whole.  


Thinking about the different activities, objects, and things in the home in terms of systems is probably a new thing for most people.  However, if you look at how you live your life you’ll discover that thinking about these activites in terms of sytems makes sense.  A fundamental assumption of H.O.M.E.S. is that most people have effective and efficient ways that they organize and manage their lives, based on their own life circumstances, needs, and values.  Over the course of life, people develop standard, habitual, system(atic) ways in which they go about the various aspects of home life, such as readying for a holiday, balancing the checkbook, cleaning the house, shopping, caring for children, etc. Assuming then, that you already have systematic ways you do things, the H.O.M.E.S. approach does not to call for an end of life as you know it.  Adopting the H.O.M.E.S. approach does not require you to scrap ways of living that you’ve already proved in your life.  Rather, the HOMES approach to living is aimed at enhancing the systems you already have.  The primary contribution that H.O.M.E.S. makes towards this end is in providing a comprehensive framework and step-by-step, activity-by-activity approach to do so.

THE AIM OF THIS BOOK—TEACH PROCESS + GIVE SPECIFIC MODEL


A major strength of H.O.M.E.S. is its broad scope.  While the orientation is towards comprehensive…
In this book I do not try to teach you all there is to know  about any single subject (eg, budgeting, menu planning, wardrobe development).  Of course no single book could ever do that, nor could all the books in the world.  Your life and preferences are unique, and therefore the answers you seek will be unique.  Therefore, my aim is to introduce you to some of the general principles of H.O.M.E.S. which will assist you as you “take-stock” of your own situation and develop a course of action custom designed for your own personal/family circumstances, values, needs, and desires.


In sum, H.O.M.E.S. can be thought of more as a cooking guide than a cookbook.  Although in the pages which follow I give you specific and practical “recipes” for organizing and managing family/personal living, my primary aim is not to tell you what you should eat or even necessarily how to prepare it, what is most important is the overall “cooking” skills and basic strategy that I can convey. 


Continuing with this metaphor, cooking, like home organization, is a creative endeavor.  You should feel free to improvise.  Do not feel bound to my recipes (systems), change them if it suits your taste or lifestyle better; change their names, pick and choose. I assume that you come to my kitchen having already established a taste for certain types of dishes and a dislike for others (some which I may have a taste for).  The important thing is that you get a feel for how my kitchen is organized, the ways I cook and then take what you will back to your own kitchen.


Thus, the HOMES approach to living empfocuses on the ways in which the different parts of everyday living are related and form a whole. HOMES provides a comprehensive, or holistic approach to organizing and managing home life.  From the HOMES perspective problems and inadequacies in one realm of life (e.g. finances) are significantly entangled with other realms of living (e.g. menu, storage) and solutions are most effective when they simultaneously attend to the different parts.  The HOMES  perspective accounts for every aspect of home and personal life.  By doing so it allows people to see the “big picture”, or the way in which one domain of life effects and is effected by other domains.   In this way it allows for more accurate assessment of the costs and benefits of implementing a given set of organization or management practices, ensures greater synergy between the different domains of home, and more provides a sound basis for working with other H.O.M.E.S. oriented people should you desire (see “Synergetic Collaboration” in chapter 2).
