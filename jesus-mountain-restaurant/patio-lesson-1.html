Jesus Mountain Restaurant · Patio Classroom · Lesson 1 prototype

Jesus, My World, My Kits

The restaurant Jesus may have been helping me build long before I knew its name.

Big question: What if the things I have been doing all along were preparation for an inheritance I am only now learning to recognize?

Before you begin: a story of pancakes

For years I have cooked and fed my family. I could describe that as household labor. But when I read Matthew 25:38–40, I can also ask whether Jesus has been letting me serve Him through the people I fed. The pancakes do not change. The story I am learning to see around them changes.

I imagine Jesus saying, “Look again at what we have already been building.” That is my sacred imaginative interpretation—not a claim that I can prove a private divine conversation. The kitchen, my family, old menu experiments, and the Food Kit may be parts of an inheritance that has been growing for decades. Jesus Mountain Restaurant is Inheritance #1 of a hoped-for hundred. It may also be the first exercise in learning how to recognize the other ninety-nine.

“Lord, when saw we thee an hungred, and fed thee?” — Matthew 25:37 (KJV)

Witting is our word for noticing the gift that was already there. Think of the Karate Kid: wax on, wax off; paint the fence; sand the floor. Training disguised as ordinary work. What if your own repetitions have also prepared you for something loving and useful?

The teaching map: from Jesus to one ordinary dinner

This is a working map, not a claim that everyone must organize life the same way. Read it as a sequence of questions: What do I believe Jesus is giving me? What is already in my world? Which practical kits help me care for it? How does food connect with everything else?

Facilitator's 55-minute lesson

1 · Welcome Jesus into the actual story (5 minutes)

Ask: “Can you remember one ordinary meal that helped someone?” Allow prayer or quiet reflection. Do not demand that anyone report a spiritual experience.

2 · Read Matthew 25, then revisit the meal (10 minutes)

Read Matthew 25:35–40. Ask who was hungry, who prepared, who cleaned, and whose burden was lightened. Explain that in this Jesus-saturated interpretation, serving people is not a lesser assignment while we wait for sacred work: it may be sacred work.

3 · Draw your world and practical kits (15 minutes)

Draw a circle marked MY WORLD. Name real people, places, abilities, recurring tasks, and resources. Around it draw kits: Food, Home, Health, Relationships, Work/Service, or your own categories. Draw at least two connecting lines. A food routine may affect money, health, family affection, and hospitality all at once.

4 · Follow the Food Kit down to a real meal (15 minutes)

Choose one meal you already know. Use the older Food Kit sequence: Plan → Gather/Store → One-time setup and routines → Food Story. Write the name of the meal, one ingredient, one routine, and one person served. The point is to recognize what exists before inventing something new.

5 · Name the inheritance and choose one action (10 minutes)

Ask: “What did you already have that you now see differently?” Call this the witting moment. Invite one small action this week—write down the pancake routine, ask a family member about a favorite dinner, label a pantry shelf, or thank someone who has been feeding you.

Facilitator check: Could the learner finish with a useful map and one real action? Did Jesus change the meaning of the work, rather than merely decorate the presentation? Did we leave the learner free to interpret their own experience?

Try it: a worked example for someone new to the Food Kit

Meet a fictional learner: Ruth lives with two relatives, cooks pasta most Tuesdays, and often feels that her kitchen is disorganized. She has not heard of “kits,” “witting,” or Jesus Mountain Restaurant. This is an invented practice case, not an actual participant.

First: name the real meal

Ruth writes: “Tuesday pasta. I cook it because my family needs dinner. My nephew washes the dishes.” She notices that the meal already contains a small act of mutual care. She does not need to invent a restaurant or buy equipment to begin.

Next: draw the world and kits

In My World she writes: “my relatives, our apartment, my job, our Tuesday dinner.” Her kits are Food (pasta and pantry), Relationships (eating together), Home (shared cleanup), and Money (a grocery limit). She draws a line from Food to Relationships, and another from Food to Money. A kit simply means an organized set of tools, information, and habits for one part of life.

Then: follow the original Food Kit

Plan: pasta for three. Gather/Store: noodles and canned tomatoes on the shelf. Setup/Routine: put a short recipe inside the cupboard door and check ingredients on Monday. Food Story: a meal where her nephew feels welcome and helps clean up.

Finally: a witting moment and one action

Ruth writes: “I thought Tuesday pasta was only a chore. It has also been a way our family stays together.” If she shares the Christian frame, she may reflect on Matthew 25 and Jesus in the people she feeds. Her one action is to write down the three-serving recipe before next Tuesday.

What this example demonstrates: the learner does not have to adopt Greg's entire theology or recreate his household. The Jesus-saturated original shows how he interprets his own story; the learner is invited, never pressured, to notice the care and gifts already present in theirs.

From your original writings

The original Food Kit is brief but explicitly names the planning, gathering/storage, routine, and Food Story structure. Greg's Top Kit uses a touchstone as a leading example in a category, with attention to current and future projects and sharing. This lesson connects those original structures to Jesus Mountain Restaurant; it does not pretend the historical documents contained this entire Jesus-saturated curriculum.
