A JESUS-GREG WORLD THOUGHT PIECE

One Hundred Late-Life Inheritances

One of them is a restaurant.

I am going to assume, as an experiment in faith and imagination, that Jesus has one hundred things He wants me to receive from Him before I die. Jesus Mountain Restaurant is one of those hoped-for inheritances. I don't mean that I have counted one hundred guaranteed possessions. I mean that I am choosing to live as though there are treasures still waiting to be discovered, received, and grown into.

Why would I want an inheritance?

Why love God? Love is much more than a bargain for rewards. Yet the language of inheritance and treasure speaks to something recognizably human: I like riches more than poverty. I want what is good, abundant, beautiful, and lasting. In the temple, in scripture, and in the promises of covenant life, inheritance is not merely something acquired. It is something one must become ready to receive. The price of a gift may be the transformation of the receiver.

Brigham Young and an inheritance larger than property

Consider Brigham Young and the early Latter-day Saint pioneers. Their settlement in the West opened an extraordinary geographic and communal inheritance, eventually stretching through Utah and into settlements far to the north and south, including Alberta and northern Mexico. Moving west also placed their central communities away from the principal battlefields of the American Civil War. This was not a simple or untroubled gift: settlement involved hardship, conflict, and the dispossession of Indigenous peoples. Still, the example helps me imagine an inheritance as a demanding new world to inhabit, not just a thing to own.

First, the preparation

Before we began building Jesus Mountain Restaurant, I sensed that Jesus had things for me to inherit, but that I needed preparation because my belief was not yet strong enough. Then came the Nanomarathon. I now understand that experience as part of my education in receiving: some gifts will be hard to accept. The world may reject them. Parts of me may reject them, too.

That is the paradox. A treasure can look like a nothing burger. Even good people, including faithful Christians, may never see what I see in it. Some might understand and wish they could believe or receive something similar. Neither response, by itself, proves or disproves the value of the gift. The experiment is whether, by staying with it and testing its fruits, I eventually recognize something genuinely precious.

Inheritance number one? A restaurant

Here beneath Horseshoe Mountain, which I call Jesus Mountain, I am learning to treat the ordinary family table as a sacred place. Menus, recipes, shopping, preparing, cooking, serving, cleaning up, teaching, remembering, welcoming: these can become the materials of Zion. The restaurant is not just a sign or a menu. It is a way of learning to inhabit a gift through the ordinary practices of family life.

What would Jesus actually do with an inheritance? I imagine Him helping people turn it into food, fellowship, learning, and love. That is the kind of restaurant I want to receive—and help build.

One hundred opportunities to learn to receive

I do not yet know the names of the other ninety-nine. Perhaps the point is not to rush to fill the list, but to become the sort of person who can recognize a gift when it arrives. Some inheritances may be small in public appearance and immense in private consequence. Some may ask more of me than I first want to give.

My hope is that, if I stay with this experiment, I will someday look back and say: Wow. Those were one hundred valuable things.

Working reflection — Greg's perspective; open to further revision and elaboration.
