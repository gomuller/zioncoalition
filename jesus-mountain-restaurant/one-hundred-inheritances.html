A JESUS-GREG WORLD THOUGHT PIECE · WORKING DRAFT

One Hundred Late-Life Inheritances

Original prototype: Jesus Mountain Restaurant (now Gift #2)

I believe Jesus has been holding treasures for me for a very long time. He has not simply been waiting for me to ask. He has been working on me—wrought upon me—preparing my imagination, my courage, my habits, and my belief so that I might finally recognize what He is offering. I have sensed Him saying, in effect: Greg, I have things for you to inherit. But I must prepare you to receive them.

So here is my late-life experiment: I am going to live as though Jesus has one hundred inheritances for me to receive before I die. I do not claim to possess a verified inventory of heaven's intentions. I am putting my faith and imagination to work, expecting treasures, watching for them, and learning how to receive them. And we are starting the numbered catalog here: #1, Jesus Mountain Restaurant.

Jesus has already been giving me gifts

This is not the first thing Jesus has given me. The Nanomarathon is one. My Jesus-Greg WORLD is another. There may already be ten or twenty such gifts whose stories we will recognize and tell as this catalog grows. But the restaurant is our first identified, numbered prototype for understanding the process. What exactly am I receiving? What must I do with it? How do I recognize its value when it looks small? How does Jesus intend to grow it with me?

Not just a gift: a gift I must grow into

Before the restaurant took shape, I sensed Jesus pressing on my belief. I needed preparation because I did not yet believe strongly enough to receive what He wanted to give. The Nanomarathon helped teach me that an inheritance from Jesus may be hard to accept. The world may reject it. Good Christians may not recognize it. Parts of me may resist it. What Jesus calls a treasure may look, to somebody else—or to yesterday's Greg—like a nothing burger.

Jesus gives me something small and simple, and then we go to work together. Not a magic beanstalk that grows without labor. A living gift: a seed, a calling, a practice, a relationship, a table. We water it, organize it, make mistakes, try again, and discover how much larger the thing can become. Jesus Mountain Restaurant begins with meals and a One Week Menu. Then the Patio Classroom grows from it. Then recipes become teaching, teaching becomes friendship, friendship becomes mutual provision, and ordinary dinner becomes a rehearsal for Zion.

The riches of inheritance

Why does the promise of inheritance get my attention? Because I like riches more than poverty. Jesus knows that. Scripture and temple language are full of treasures, promises, kingdoms, and inheritance. Love of God is more than a transaction, but perhaps God knows how to attract His children toward good things they do not yet know how to desire. The price is not always money. Sometimes the price is becoming the person who can bear the gift.

Brigham Young: a difficult inheritance

I think of Brigham Young and the early Latter-day Saints, whose western settlements eventually reached beyond Utah toward Alberta and northern Mexico. Their move also placed their principal settlements away from the central battlefields of the American Civil War. Their history includes immense hardship and serious consequences for Indigenous peoples. Still, in my spiritual reading of their story, I see an inheritance that required people to travel, labor, organize, endure, and learn to inhabit an unfamiliar world. A great inheritance is not necessarily an easy one.

Redeemed with a Story: Jesus was already here

I suspect many—perhaps all—of these hundred late-life inheritances will be redemption material. Jesus will take something that has been here all along and give it back to me with a new story, a new order, and more of Himself in it. That is what I do with songs: the familiar tune remains, much of the old feeling remains, sometimes even much of the lyric remains, but the song is changed. It has more Jesus. It becomes a new inheritance without ceasing to have a history.

Perhaps the same is true of the restaurant. Food was already here. Family dinner was already here. Recipes, shopping lists, gardens, neighbors, the mountain, the ordinary work of feeding people—all here. Jesus does not have to invent these things from nothing. He can redeem them with a story. He can show me the Zion that has been hiding in plain sight.

The wheat of Zion is already growing

I am beginning to anticipate that the roots of Zion have been here all along. There may be pruning. There may be sorting, separating wheat from tares, removing what does not nourish life. But the wheat is already here. We can recognize it, gather it, process it in little units, and learn how to live with it. A dinner can be one unit. A Food Kit another. A song, a memory, a neighbor, a classroom, a family practice: each may be a small piece of Zion ready for Jesus to bring into greater order.

Jesus, show me what Zion is like

In my imagination, I ask: Jesus, do You want to show me what Zion is going to be like? And His answer is not merely an explanation. It is an invitation: Come. Let's enact a little of it together. That is how I understand Jesus Mountain Restaurant. It is a prototype—an approximation, a glimpse, a little enacted prophecy. I don't know that restaurants in Zion will look exactly like this. But I expect they will be more like this than the restaurants we commonly know today: more generous with knowledge, more devoted to people, more cooperative, more integrated, more Jesus.

Things as they were, things as they are, and things as they are to come. The restaurant gives me a way to look backward at the good that has already existed, attend to what Jesus is doing with it now, and catch a peek of what might yet come. That peek delights me. It makes me love Jesus more.

More order, more Jesus, more coherence

And Jesus is not giving me only a preview. He is helping me put my actual life in order. My menus become simpler. My habits become more coherent. My memories find places in a story. My desire to teach others finds a classroom—even if the class ultimately includes only one or two mortal students. The angels can attend, too. What matters is that I learn to recognize and share what Jesus is teaching me.

Perhaps someday a nonprofit, a graduate student, a young entrepreneur, or a neighbor will take this framework and run with it. They may use less explicit Jesus language. They may translate it into a community food system, an educational program, or a practical guide to family organization. I would welcome that. Let the good spread throughout the earth. Here, in the Jesus-Greg WORLD prototype, we are keeping the Jesus saturation turned all the way up, so we can see what grows.

Witting: the treasure I already had

Here is another kind of redemption Jesus may be teaching me: the inheritance of becoming witting. Unwittingly, I may already have been living with a gift. Wittingly, I finally see it. I receive not merely the thing, but eyes to recognize the thing—and then the thing itself becomes new to me.

For thirty-six years I have lived with my wife. Suppose I ask Jesus for a wonderful person to share my life, and then suddenly recognize the wonderful person He has already given me. She is not a new arrival. My gratitude is. And the old blessing becomes a new treasure because I finally know what I am looking at.

I think of Daniel in The Karate Kid, discovering that the chores and movements he had been practicing were training. The work was real before he understood it; the revelation makes him ready to use it. That is a kind of inheritance: not simply new capacity, but new understanding of capacity already being cultivated.

I also think about the promise in Doctrine and Covenants 89 to run and not be weary, to walk and not faint. As I age and feel weariness in my own body, I wonder whether part of the blessing I need to explore is not only physical strength but eyes to see the ways Jesus has sustained me. That is a personal reading and a question, not a denial of the ordinary realities of aging or of the scripture's other meanings.

Recognizing Jesus in the music that was already here

Sometimes I hear a familiar rock song and imagine Jesus was at work in the human story around its creation—even if the musicians never described it that way. I cannot know their private spiritual experiences. But I can ask what becomes possible when I hear the song through my relationship with Jesus. I can sing it, redeem its meaning in my own life, sometimes write new words, and receive it as something newly precious. The tune was here. The feeling was here. Now I can hear more Jesus in it.

That is part of the long thread I have been trying to hold at ZionCoalition.org: the possibility that Jesus has been working in and through ordinary people, culture, music, labor, and love in ways I did not previously recognize. I do not need everyone to share my interpretation to receive the delight it gives me.

For me, for a few, and for a multitude beyond me

I have wanted to be the one who spreads the news—the One Week Menu guru, the first to announce the discovery. I can feel that desire in myself. But I am learning to imagine a more generous calling: Jesus may be showing me a prototype of something that others will build and teach on a much larger scale. My work may reach one or two mortal students, perhaps more. I do not need to own the movement or be first to the world.

If other people take the lessons farther, I can rejoice. I can go build the next small thing Jesus and I are working on. And in the sacred imagination of my Jesus-Greg WORLD, the room is never merely occupied by one or two: there is room for a multitude of angels, too.

Perhaps inheritance is not just receiving more. Perhaps it is seeing more of what Jesus has already given—and learning to share it without needing to possess the spotlight.

The restaurant was here all along: Matthew 25

Here is the revelation that brings me back to Jesus Mountain Restaurant: I may have been running the foundations of Jesus Mountain Restaurant for my entire married life without knowing its name. In Matthew 25:38–40, people ask Jesus when they saw Him hungry and fed Him. He answers that what they did for others, they did for Him. What if that is not merely a distant lesson about charity, but a way of seeing the pancakes I made, the meals we shared, and the ordinary burdens we carried for one another?

I imagine Jesus saying: Greg, you've been serving at My restaurant for years. You thought you were just making breakfast. You were learning to feed Me by feeding the people I love. The family kitchen was not a failed or lesser version of a restaurant. It was the preparation, the practice, perhaps even the first dining room.

In my religious imagination, I recognize a pattern of long preparation: believers see God's purposes unfolding through generations before the mortal ministry of Christ, and Latter-day Saints see histories and circumstances preparing the way long before Joseph Smith was born. Perhaps I can learn to look backward at my own small history with similar gratitude, without claiming to know every detail of God's intentions. The roots may have been growing for decades before I recognized the tree.

So Inheritance #1 may be both new and old. The website, the name, the menu, and the Patio Classroom are new. But the habit of feeding family, bearing burdens, and gathering around a table has been here all along. Jesus has given me new eyes to see the old work—and an invitation to do it more wittingly from now on.

And perhaps that question belongs to every visitor: What if you've already been running a little Zion restaurant in your own home? Not because your kitchen is perfect, but because love has been served there. What might Jesus help you recognize, redeem, and grow?

A test for Jesus saturation

If I could remove Jesus from Jesus Mountain Restaurant and leave the meaning of the restaurant essentially unchanged, then I have missed what I believe He is giving me. This is not simply a menu system with religious decorations. Jesus is the reason I am looking backward at thirty-six years of family meals as preparation; the reason Matthew 25 turns a pancake into a possibility for serving Him; the reason I can imagine my wife, my children, my neighbors, and the unseen multitude at the table; and the reason I want to give away the recipes without insisting that I be the guru.

Each inheritance can have a practical object and a spiritual recognition: the song and the new hearing, the meal and the new eyes, the Food Kit and the new order, the classroom and the new willingness to teach one person. That is how I want to tell these stories: not by attaching Jesus to them afterward, but by asking what I believe Jesus has been doing with me in them all along.

One hundred, but perhaps one world

I suspect Jesus is not assembling one hundred disconnected prizes. His pattern, as I am beginning to see it, is integration. The Nanomarathon trains belief. Jesus-Greg WORLD gives the story a home. The restaurant gives that story a table, a kitchen, a menu, a Food Kit, and a Patio Classroom. Each gift reinforces the others. One hundred inheritances may eventually become one richly interconnected world.

So my eyes are open. Jesus, what else have You been keeping for me? What else are You preparing me to receive? What will we grow together next?

For now, we begin with the first named inheritance. Come to dinner. Jesus is already at the table.

This is Greg's faith-filled first-draft interpretation, deliberately written with maximum Jesus saturation. We can refine it as the story unfolds.
