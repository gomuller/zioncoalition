Explorations of Charity

“Charity suffereth long, and is kind.” — 1 Corinthians 13:4

What if charity is not only something we show to people, but a practical principle we can investigate in nearly every realm of life?

The Geography of Charity

We usually speak of charity as something we show to other people. But what if charity also describes the way we meet difficult tasks, demanding institutions, unfamiliar ideas, and the ordinary resistance of life? I want to investigate a practical question: what are the implications of charity, and what are its limitations?

People seem to have very different territories in which they can suffer long and remain kind. Aptitude, experience, temperament, beliefs, incentives, and love may all help explain these differences.

Someone can patiently spend years repairing engines, caring for children, or building a home, yet lose patience with a government application. Another person can calmly complete the application but struggle to listen for ten minutes to someone with opposing beliefs.

Perhaps each of us has a geography of charity: a map of the places where we are willing to give sustained, generous attention.

The FAFSA Revelation

My daughter confronted the complexity of a Medicaid application and found it overwhelming. It is easy to simply stop at that hurdle. The forms were too complicated, too long, too aggravating.

I remember my own experience completing college financial-aid applications. I reframed the hours of paperwork in economic terms. If several hours of careful work could produce thousands of dollars in Pell Grants, loans, or other aid, the economic value of those hours could indeed be substantial.

Suddenly I could approach each question patiently and carefully. I had found a reason to exercise what I began to recognize as charity for the process.

Of course, a financial incentive is not the same thing as the pure love of Christ. Yet it may cultivate capacities associated with charity: patience, attentiveness, humility, and endurance. That distinction is important.

Grant Writing as an Exercise in Charity

I once taught grant writing. If I taught it again, I might build the course around charity.

A grant application is a long series of questions. Behind those questions is often a real person or committee trying to decide whether to trust an applicant with resources. They do not yet know the applicant, and before they can trust the applicant, they need a story they can understand.

The writer can resent each question or answer with generosity: What is this person actually trying to understand? What would help them make a responsible decision?

Such patience does not replace a sound proposal or honest evidence. It can improve the quality of the work. It does not guarantee a grant, but it can improve the quality of the application.

The question is bigger than grants: when somebody asks something demanding of us, we may have an opportunity to respond with greater generosity of attention.

Scouts, Pioneers, and Settlers

Think of scouts, pioneers, and settlers. The scout can endure uncertainty, exposure, and the frustration of finding a way through unfamiliar country. The pioneer can endure years of difficult labor establishing irrigation, roads, farms, and homes. The settler may excel at sustaining a community, stabilizing its institutions, and improving what had already been established.

These are not identical forms of long-suffering. A person who cannot tolerate one of them is not necessarily less virtuous. It may mean their capacity for endurance belongs to a different territory.

This suggests a practical humility: do not confuse one person's preferred form of endurance with universal moral superiority.

Charity and Economic Life

Everybody has to have charity for something if they are going to make money—or at least they must be willing to persist through some kind of difficulty that others need addressed. An accountant endures the details. A mechanic patiently diagnoses a stubborn failure. A caregiver attends to the same need again and again. A researcher pursues a question for years.

The willingness to do difficult work can create economic value. But competence, incentives, and Christian charity are not identical: a person can be excellent at a job and hate every minute of it.

Still, vocational fit may partly involve discovering the kinds of difficulties we can meet with sustained attention. There is something worth exploring in the possibility that finding one's work involves finding where one can willingly suffer long.

The Limits of Charity

Charity cannot mean an unlimited obligation to endure everything. Capacity, willingness, and wisdom are different questions. Fatigue is real; exploitation is real; enduring a harmful arrangement indefinitely is not always the charitable thing to do.

And charity should not be demanded only of the person facing a difficult system. The administrators who require elaborate applications may themselves need charity for the applicants. They could express it by making the process simpler.

Sometimes charity means patiently helping somebody across the hurdle. Sometimes it means removing the hurdle.

Expanding the Territory

Could we deliberately practice charity toward a task, an institution, a belief, or an ordinary routine? Could we expand our patience without mistaking mere stubbornness for virtue? What would it mean to have charity toward oneself without turning self-compassion into self-excuse?

These questions belong in Jesus-Greg WORLD because the experiment is concerned with the recurring mechanics of life, not merely admiring them at the level of abstraction.

I want to discover whether I can expand the territory in which I suffer long, remain kind, and offer generous attention.

Moroni 7:47 calls charity the pure love of Christ. How does that sacred meaning relate to capacities arising from temperament, duty, ambition, and love? That is not a distinction to erase casually.

My working hypothesis is that the capacities associated with charity differ across the territories of life. By understanding that geography and extending that capacity with wisdom, we may enlarge not only our usefulness but our world.

Intellectual Charity: Toward Ideas

Charity toward a person is not necessarily charity toward that person's ideas. I may be pleasant to someone whose religious or political claims I find absurd, while refusing to spend even ten minutes understanding the claims themselves. What does that tell me about the boundaries of my charity?

In philosophy and argument, the principle of charity asks us to interpret another person's position in its strongest reasonable form before criticizing it. That principle is not identical to Moroni's pure love of Christ, but it offers a revealing practical analogue: patience toward the thought, not merely courtesy toward its thinker.

Charity for an idea does not require agreeing with it. It can mean asking better questions, resisting caricature, and making the effort to understand before dismissing. It may be one of the disciplines through which truth-seeking becomes less governed by ego and haste.

Charity in Leadership and Institutions

Now reverse the direction of the question. We have asked whether the applicant has charity for the Medicaid form. Does the institution have charity for the applicant?

An institution may demand enormous patience from the people it serves while offering little patience in return. A workplace may expect employees to endure management's mistakes but punish employees for their own. A leader may say that people should be resilient while creating conditions in which mistakes are humiliating.

Charity can therefore be more than an individual's disposition. It can become a property of an environment: clear instructions, fair opportunities to correct errors, patient listening, and the freedom to learn without unnecessary fear. Such conditions can help people take responsible risks and do better work.

Perhaps one measure of a charitable institution is not how much suffering it can demand, but how much needless suffering it is willing to remove.

Charity Toward Failure

Suppose I am repairing a machine and my first three attempts fail. I can become resentful of the machine, the instructions, and myself. Or I can become curious: What did that attempt teach me? What have I not yet understood?

Long-suffering by itself is not always enough. A person may patiently repeat the same mistake for years. But patience joined to humility, kindness, and inquiry makes continued effort intelligent. It turns failure into information rather than a personal insult.

This suggests another practical question: does charity change only how long I keep going, or also the quality of attention I bring to the next attempt?

Charity Toward Myself

The geography of charity includes my relationship with myself. I may patiently explain a mistake to another person and mercilessly condemn myself for making the same mistake. I may demand an impossible standard of performance and then interpret exhaustion as a moral failure.

Charity toward myself would not mean excusing everything. It might mean telling the truth without cruelty, recognizing limits of capacity, and making a sustainable next attempt. It could help distinguish repentance and improvement from self-punishment.

Perfect in That Thing: Alma 32 and the Particularity of Charity

Alma asks a remarkable question: “Is your knowledge perfect?” And answers, “Yea, your knowledge is perfect in that thing” (Alma 32:34).

In that thing.

What a liberating qualification! I need not possess perfect charity to obtain perfect knowledge in a particular thing—knowledge that a particular seed is good.

I may discover how to suffer long and remain kind while completing a government application. I may learn something genuine about charity toward a particular person, an unfamiliar idea, or a stubborn machine.

That discovery does not make me universally charitable. It does not even guarantee that I will exercise charity successfully in the same circumstances tomorrow. But I have learned something. The seed has swollen. My understanding has been enlarged.

This suggests two complementary principles:

Incrementalism: My capacity for charity can grow through successive experiments.

Particularism: Each experiment can yield genuine knowledge within its own particular territory, without requiring universal mastery.

Indeed, particularism may be even more important than incrementalism. I need not wait until I have become charitable everywhere to discover charity somewhere.

And that gives my Jesus-Greg WORLD experiment a wonderfully practical direction. Rather than asking only, “How can I become more charitable?” I can ask:

What particular thing shall I exercise charity toward today? And what might I come to know in that thing?

Alma reminds us that even after discovering the seed is good, we must continue nourishing the tree. The experiment continues. But its discoveries are already real.

Importantly, Alma's perfect knowledge concerns knowing that the seed is good—not a claim that I have exercised charity perfectly.

Can Charity Cross Borders?

The most intriguing question may not be whether I have charity, but whether I can transfer it. Why can I endure years of one kind of difficulty yet barely tolerate minutes of another? What makes one territory inviting and another unbearable?

If I can patiently complete a FAFSA after discovering its value, could I learn a similar generosity of attention toward an opposing idea, a difficult conversation, a malfunctioning tool, or a tedious household routine?

Is charity a finite resource to allocate wisely, a capacity that grows through deliberate exercise, or both? When does practice enlarge my territory, and when do exhaustion and real limitations call for rest or a change of method?

This is the ongoing experiment: to practice charity incrementally, to discover its goodness particularly—in that thing—and to learn how it operates, where it fails, and how—under the influence of Christ—it might be cultivated across the ordinary territories of my life.
