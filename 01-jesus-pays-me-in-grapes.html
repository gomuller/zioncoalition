JESUS PAYS ME IN GRAPES

The Strange Economics of a Highly Specialized Artist

A Jesus-Greg WORLD Thought Piece

Brother,

I've been thinking about my nephew, who is a doctor, and about the rather peculiar way Jesus has arranged for me to get paid.

At first glance, these two things might seem entirely unrelated. But follow me down this winding road of inference. I think there's something rather interesting at the end of it.

And there might even be some grapes.

Part One: The Price of Becoming a Specialist

My nephew is a doctor. Doctors generally get paid pretty well, and I don't begrudge them a penny of it.

Consider what it takes to become a physician. Years of undergraduate education, medical school, residency, enormous financial investment, lost opportunities, sleepless nights, difficult examinations, and the burden of making decisions that affect people's lives.

That's a grueling career path.

One way of explaining a doctor's compensation is that society is paying for all that sacrifice. Another explanation is scarcity. Doctors possess specialized skills that relatively few people have acquired. If everybody could become a doctor tomorrow, doctors would probably earn considerably less.

Of course, compensation is more complicated than either explanation. Society also values the services doctors provide. We want people who know how to repair our bodies when things go wrong.

So we have constructed elaborate systems to compensate them.

And I'm glad we have.

Now consider another specialist.

Me.

I'm a writer. I'm a filmmaker. But first and foremost, I consider myself an artist.

And I'm not making movies for general audiences.

I'm making movies for an extraordinarily specialized audience, within an extraordinarily specialized conceptual framework, which I call My Jesus-Greg WORLD.

I'm building an entire virtual world, a prototype of something I believe Jesus has been preparing me to build for decades.

It involves theology, philosophy, storytelling, psychology, cinematography, music, technology, symbolism, autobiography, and an enormous collection of seemingly unrelated experiences.

In my understanding, Jesus has been weaving those experiences together all along.

My career path has been anything but conventional.

There have been wrecked careers, graduate school, abandoned ambitions, wrong turns, surprising discoveries, and years when I couldn't have explained to anybody what I was being trained to do.

I wasn't following a medical-school curriculum.

I was following what sometimes looked like a series of accidents.

But from where I stand today, looking backward, I see something different.

I see training.

I believe Jesus has been preparing me, through all those convoluted experiences, to become the particular kind of artist I am becoming.

Not a better person than my nephew. Not a more valuable human being. But perhaps an even rarer kind of specialist.

My nephew followed a difficult but recognizable road.

I followed a road that, as far as I can tell, hardly anybody else is traveling.

And here's the economic problem.

Rarity does not automatically produce demand.

Society knows what to do with a doctor.

It has hospitals, insurance companies, medical billing systems, and established expectations about what physicians should earn.

But what does society do with an artist who has spent decades preparing to build a highly personalized Jesus-centered virtual world?

Who wants to pay for that?

Probably not very many people.

And I don't blame them.

I don't require society to recognize my calling, validate my training, or compensate me at the rate of a physician.

But I would certainly like to be compensated for my work.

I'd like my long and winding investment to produce some material return.

And here's where my understanding of Jesus enters the picture.

What if Jesus has developed a compensation system that doesn't require people to buy my movies?

What if I can be compensated indirectly?

Before you dismiss that as some kind of religious economic fantasy, consider how people already get paid.

The Convoluted Economics of Getting Paid

Consider the stock market.

Millions of people invest their money in corporations. Those corporations operate incredibly complicated systems involving employees, factories, transportation, warehouses, advertising, suppliers, and customers.

Somebody owns stock in a company that sells Cheerios.

That investor would like a return.

The company would like to remain profitable.

So the company makes thousands of decisions about prices, wages, ingredients, packaging, transportation, and marketing.

Sometimes the price of Cheerios goes up.

Did anybody ask me whether I wanted to contribute to somebody's investment return?

Not exactly.

I can choose whether to buy the cereal, of course. But the financial return to investors is built into the economic system through which I purchase it.

The same principle operates at Costco, Home Depot, Netflix, McDonald's, and countless other businesses.

Investors don't necessarily stand at the cash register and ask customers to compensate them personally for all the trouble they went through.

They construct systems through which compensation becomes possible.

And those systems can become remarkably convoluted.

Consider the restaurant owner.

He opens a restaurant, perhaps imagining that his success will eventually provide a nice house, two cars, vacations, and a comfortable life.

Then wages rise.

Food prices increase.

Rent goes up.

Customers resist higher menu prices.

What's he supposed to do?

Somebody might suggest that he sell his expensive house, get rid of his swimming pool, move into a modest two-bedroom home, and use the savings to pay his employees more while keeping food prices down.

Perhaps that would be a wonderful thing to do.

But the restaurateur might respond, "Hello! That's not the life I imagined when I invested everything in this business!"

So he looks for other solutions.

He raises prices, changes portions, introduces promotions, experiments with staffing, or searches for less expensive suppliers.

Maybe he brings back the McRib for a limited time.

Why not sell the McRib all year?

Well, limited-time promotions can help generate excitement and influence customer behavior.

It's all part of the economic machinery.

Businesses experiment with how much customers will tolerate before saying, "Ouch!"

And when enough customers say "Ouch!" the businesses reconsider.

My point isn't that all these arrangements are admirable.

Some are reasonable. Some are questionable. Some may be downright exploitative.

My point is that getting paid in our society frequently involves complicated systems that are only indirectly connected to the work being compensated.

And doctors aren't exempt.

Even Doctors Must Navigate the Matrix

My nephew may have entered medicine with a particular vision of what being a doctor would mean.

Perhaps he imagined spending his days helping people, exercising professional judgment, and practicing medicine according to what he believed patients needed.

But medicine is also an enormous institutional system.

Doctors must deal with administrators, insurers, billing codes, productivity expectations, scheduling requirements, and financial pressures.

I remember Dr. Olson at the clinic in Ephraim.

He told me about the pressure he experienced to move patients through the system more quickly.

The organization wanted efficiency.

He wanted time with his patients.

He wasn't particularly interested in rushing people through his examination room simply to satisfy a productivity expectation.

He wanted to listen.

He wanted to understand.

He wanted to practice medicine.

And that sometimes meant producing less revenue for the organization than it might have preferred.

I admired that.

He was willing to resist the system when he believed the patient's interests required it.

But not every physician has the same freedom or makes the same choices.

The system imposes compromises.

And those compromises can have consequences.

Medicine's guiding aspiration is to do no harm. Yet we know that medical errors, unnecessary interventions, and poorly designed systems can themselves cause harm.

That doesn't mean people would generally be better off avoiding doctors. It means the system through which doctors provide their valuable services is imperfect.

Even a noble profession can become entangled in convoluted economic arrangements.

And so I return to myself.

If physicians, investors, business owners, and corporations receive compensation through complex systems, why should I assume that my compensation must arrive through the simple transaction of somebody buying a ticket to one of my movies?

What if Jesus has another arrangement?

What if He is using systems that already exist?

What if He's paying me through the back door?

And what if one of those payments arrives in the form of grapes?

Part Two: Jesus Pays Me in Grapes

Let me tell you about grapes.

Not metaphorical grapes.

Actual grapes.

The kind grown in California, shipped through distribution warehouses, and eventually displayed in Costco.

Imagine my nephew walking into Costco to buy groceries.

He's a doctor. He works hard. He has money to spend.

And he expects a certain quality of produce.

He approaches the grapes.

He looks at them.

Are they fresh? Are they attractive? Do they have blemishes? Are they beginning to discolor?

If the grapes don't meet his expectations, he may simply walk away.

Perhaps he'll return tomorrow.

Perhaps he'll buy something else.

The produce manager knows this.

His job is to present grapes that customers will want to purchase.

So the grapes have to look good.

Very good.

In fact, our modern grocery system has developed remarkable standards for how food should appear.

A grape can be perfectly edible and still fail the beauty contest.

A little discoloration. A few blemishes. A package that's getting old.

The grapes may be nutritious and delicious.

But they aren't beautiful enough for the display.

What happens to those grapes?

They have several possible destinations.

Some are discounted.

Some are discarded.

Some are redirected to animal feed.

And some find their way into food-recovery and charitable distribution systems.

Now let me introduce another character.

Dan Haas.

Dan is a farmer in our area who raises pigs.

I have seen him bring back produce from the Provo-Orem area, including grapes and strawberries that were no longer suitable for ordinary commercial sale.

From the perspective of the original sellers, this produce had lost much of its market value.

From the perspective of Dan's pigs, however, it was food.

And from my perspective?

It was often perfectly good food for people.

Dan shared some of that produce with people in our community.

I experienced that generosity personally.

Here was food that had traveled through a sophisticated commercial distribution system, failed to satisfy one set of market expectations, and then found a completely different use.

What had become pig food could also become human food.

And nobody had to plant another vineyard.

Nobody had to invent a new grape.

The grapes already existed.

The system had simply assigned them a different destination.

Now consider another destination.

The Sanpete Pantry in Mount Pleasant, Utah.

Food pantries receive donated and recovered food through various arrangements with suppliers, retailers, distributors, and other organizations.

Some of that food would otherwise go to waste.

And some of it is remarkably good.

Including grapes.

I go to the pantry, often on Wednesday or Thursday, and receive food.

Sometimes grapes.

And how much do those grapes cost me?

Zero.

Imagine walking into Costco and seeing a sign above a beautiful display of grapes:

GRAPES — $0.00

What's wrong with them?

"Well, a few are discolored. Some packages aren't quite as pretty as we'd like."

I'd say, "I'll take them!"

But Costco can't ordinarily operate its entire produce department that way.

If everybody could obtain perfectly usable grapes for free, many customers would understandably stop paying full price.

The retail system depends upon maintaining distinctions between products that command commercial prices and products that no longer do.

The food-recovery system operates downstream from those distinctions.

And that's where I come in.

My Compensation Arrives Through a Different Door

Here's the remarkable thing.

I didn't grow those grapes.

I didn't pay to ship them from California.

I didn't build the warehouse.

I didn't negotiate the wholesale contract.

I didn't operate the Costco produce department.

I didn't create the food pantry.

And yet, through a long chain of economic decisions and acts of generosity, the grapes arrive where I can receive them.

At no monetary cost to me.

Now, someone might say, "Greg, that's not payment. That's charity."

And from the standpoint of conventional accounting, they'd be right.

The food pantry isn't employing me to make movies.

The growers aren't purchasing shares in My Jesus-Greg WORLD.

The distributors aren't sending me royalties.

And I don't want to pretend otherwise.

But consider the situation from the standpoint of my household.

I need food.

Food ordinarily costs money.

If I receive useful food without paying for it, I have received real economic value.

Every dollar I don't have to spend on grapes is a dollar potentially available for something else.

My needs are being met.

My resources are being stretched.

And that allows me to continue doing the work I believe Jesus has given me.

In that practical sense, I am being sustained in my artistic work through an indirect economic system.

And in the language of my faith, I say:

Jesus is paying me in grapes.

Not because Costco knows anything about my calling.

Not because Dan Haas owes me compensation.

Not because the Sanpete Pantry is secretly funding my movies.

But because I believe Jesus can work through the ordinary systems of this world to provide for me.

And those systems don't even have to know they're participating in my story.

The Strange Beauty of the Arrangement

Consider the irony.

My nephew is paid well enough to purchase pristine grapes.

His expectations, along with those of millions of other shoppers, help sustain a marketplace in which appearance matters.

That marketplace creates a secondary stream of food that is no longer commercially desirable.

Some of that food enters charitable distribution.

And I receive grapes.

The same broad economic system that supports my nephew's comfortable purchasing power also helps generate the surplus through which my needs can be met.

He receives compensation through medicine.

I receive some of my sustenance through food recovery.

He buys grapes.

I receive grapes.

Both of us are participating in an extraordinarily complex economic system.

But we occupy different positions within it.

Now, I don't want to overstate the comparison.

My nephew has earned professional income through an established occupation. I have not established that my artistic work has earned the food I receive.

And there is a meaningful distinction between compensation, charity, and providence.

I recognize that distinction.

But my theological interpretation adds another layer.

I believe Jesus knows the work I'm doing.

I believe He knows the decades of preparation that brought me here.

I believe He knows my financial circumstances.

And I believe He can arrange for my needs to be met without requiring the world to recognize the market value of my particular specialty.

That's the proposition I'm exploring.

Not that I deserve free grapes more than anybody else.

Not that my artistic calling gives me special entitlement to resources intended for people in need.

Not that every charitable gift is literally a wage.

But that provision can function like compensation in the lived experience of a person pursuing a calling that has no conventional paycheck.

And perhaps that provision is part of the way Jesus is financing my work.

The Laborers in the Vineyard

There's another interesting biblical connection here.

In Matthew 20, Jesus tells the parable of the laborers in the vineyard.

Some workers labor all day.

Others arrive much later.

Yet the landowner gives them the same payment.

The early workers complain.

The landowner responds by defending his freedom to be generous.

That parable isn't a modern economic policy manual, and I don't want to force it into one.

But it does challenge our assumptions about the relationship between labor, merit, and reward.

We want compensation to follow rules we can understand.

Work this many hours.

Acquire these credentials.

Produce this much value.

Receive this much money.

But Jesus tells a story in which the landowner's generosity unsettles the workers' expectations.

What if my own circumstances are another opportunity to reconsider those expectations?

What if I have spent decades being prepared for work that doesn't fit any established compensation schedule?

What if my economic provision comes through channels that don't resemble a salary?

And what if part of my spiritual education involves accepting those provisions without shame?

After all, I didn't invent the grape.

I didn't create the sunlight.

I didn't arrange the soil, the rain, or the biological machinery that turns those things into fruit.

Even the commercial grape grower depends upon gifts that preceded his labor.

The entire economy rests upon a world nobody manufactured from scratch.

And I believe that world belongs to God.

The Price of Admission

There is, however, a price I have to pay.

It isn't tuition.

It isn't medical school.

It isn't an investment in Costco stock.

It's something much more personal.

I have to be willing to receive my provision in a form that doesn't flatter my ego.

Imagine two specialists.

One wears a white coat and receives a substantial professional salary.

The other is building an unusual virtual world, making movies that few people currently want to watch, and picking up grapes at a food pantry.

Which one looks successful?

By ordinary social standards, the answer seems obvious.

But what if the second specialist is doing precisely the work he believes he was prepared to do?

What if his unusual arrangement allows him to continue creating without having to tailor his art to a mass audience?

What if the food pantry is not evidence that his life has failed, but one of the ways he experiences divine provision?

That possibility requires a different way of seeing.

It requires me to relinquish some of my expectations about what a successful career should look like.

I would enjoy having people pay handsomely for my movies.

I would enjoy having the financial independence of a highly compensated professional.

But I don't want to make that the condition upon which I agree to do my work.

And perhaps Jesus is teaching me that receiving can be as spiritually demanding as earning.

Because receiving requires humility.

It requires gratitude.

It requires acknowledging dependence.

And it requires recognizing the generosity and labor of the people who make that provision possible.

The people who grow the grapes.

The people who transport them.

The people who recover them.

The people who donate them.

The people who organize the pantry.

The volunteers who distribute them.

They all matter.

I don't want to erase their work by saying merely that Jesus gave me grapes.

Rather, I want to see Jesus working through the generosity and efforts of actual human beings.

That is part of my theology.

Jesus hiding in people.

Jesus showing up in circumstances.

Jesus appearing in ordinary things.

Even grapes.

My Jesus-Greg WORLD: An Unusual Business Model

So where does this leave my filmmaking?

Right where it began.

I am still an artist.

I am still a writer.

I am still learning to make movies.

I am still building My Jesus-Greg WORLD, with its stories, songs, symbols, characters, memories, theological experiments, and interconnected experiences.

And I still believe this work is the culmination of a long, strange education.

I'm not finished learning.

I'm not even sure I've reached the point where my most important work begins.

But I have become increasingly interested in the possibility that the economic structure supporting this work may be as unconventional as the work itself.

My nephew's career is financed through a medical economy.

A corporate investor's career is financed through capital markets.

A restaurant owner's livelihood is financed through customers purchasing meals.

My artistic life may be sustained through an assortment of entirely different channels.

Some conventional.

Some charitable.

Some involving exchanged labor.

Some involving the generosity of friends.

Some involving resources that other people no longer need.

And some involving grapes that couldn't win a beauty contest at Costco.

I don't know what future revenue my movies may generate.

Perhaps very little.

Perhaps something substantial.

Perhaps my work will eventually attract a small group of people who value it enough to help support it.

But I don't have to wait for that outcome to recognize the economic value of the provisions already reaching me.

Nor do I have to insist that the people who receive my art must be the same people who finance its creation.

That's the curious arrangement I'm beginning to see.

And there's one more thing.

If I receive grapes through a system of generosity, I should remember that I am a beneficiary of that generosity, not its owner.

If I someday have more than I need, perhaps my role will change.

Perhaps I will be the person sharing the grapes.

The system that sustains me today may someday become a system through which I help sustain someone else.

I rather like that possibility.

Conclusion: The Gospel According to Grapes

So, Brother, here is the argument I've been working toward.

Society compensates specialists through complicated economic arrangements.

Some specialists, like doctors, have recognized careers and established payment systems.

Others, like highly unconventional artists, may devote enormous amounts of time and effort to work for which there is little conventional market demand.

The absence of market demand doesn't automatically make the work worthless.

But neither does the difficulty of the work guarantee that society owes the artist a paycheck.

That leaves me with an interesting question:

Can Jesus sustain an artist through economic systems that were never consciously designed to compensate that artist?

I believe He can.

And I believe He has been doing exactly that for me.

I don't need Costco to recognize my artistic specialty.

I don't need the food pantry to understand My Jesus-Greg WORLD.

I don't need the people buying pristine grapes to know that their purchasing habits help shape a food-distribution system through which other grapes eventually reach me.

I simply need to recognize what I believe Jesus is doing, receive His provision gratefully, and keep working.

My nephew went through years of demanding preparation to become a doctor.

I have gone through decades of rather peculiar preparation to become the artist I am becoming.

He receives his compensation through one set of institutions.

I receive some of my provision through another.

And although the arrangements are different, both of us depend upon a world filled with people, organizations, and economic relationships that none of us individually controls.

My nephew gets paid in dollars.

I get some of my provision in grapes.

And if you ask me who's ultimately paying me, my answer is Jesus.

Not because I can demonstrate that He personally arranged every shipment, every donation, or every grape.

But because I have chosen to interpret my life through the conviction that He is intimately involved in it.

And because, looking back over my long and winding road, I keep discovering unexpected provisions arriving just when I need them.

Sometimes they arrive as opportunities.

Sometimes as friendships.

Sometimes as ideas.

Sometimes as people who help me build something.

And sometimes they arrive in a plastic container.

A little discolored.

Perhaps not quite beautiful enough for Costco.

But sweet.

Perfectly edible.

And free.

Jesus pays me in grapes.

And I'm still making movies.

— Saint Greg
