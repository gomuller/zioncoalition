The Farm Boy and the Future

I believe we are approaching a time when God will astonish us with what ordinary people can accomplish through extraordinary gifts.

And there is precedent.

Consider Joseph Smith.

A young farm boy. Little formal education. No university training in ancient languages. No credentials that would qualify him to translate an ancient religious record.

🎵 I believe we are approaching an extraordinary period in human history when ordinary people will receive two great inheritances. The first is artificial intelligence, giving almost everyone access to extraordinary intellectual powers previously requiring years of education, training, and expertise. The second, corresponding inheritance will be an unprecedented outpouring of the gifts of God through the Holy Ghost. Wisdom, discernment, faith, love, creativity, and spiritual power will become available in extraordinary measure. AI will dramatically expand what human beings can do. God's gifts will transform what human beings can become. I believe these two inheritances will help usher in Zion.

Yet Joseph testified that he translated the Book of Mormon by the gift and power of God.

Think about the implications.

What would ordinarily require years of specialized preparation was, according to his testimony, accomplished through divine assistance.

Now consider the coming Zion.

What if God is preparing to pour out His Spirit in exceptional abundance? What if extraordinary wisdom, discernment, understanding, and capacities for loving and serving others become available in ways we have scarcely imagined?

Not because everyone suddenly becomes a scholar.

Not because human preparation ceases to matter.

But because God gives gifts.

I cannot tell you when or how extensively this will happen. This is my sacred guess, my Alma 32 experiment in believing.

But I sense that the latter-day glories are only beginning.

And when I contemplate that young farm boy translating scripture, I find myself wondering:

What if Joseph Smith was not merely an exception, but a preview?

Joseph Smith's experience raises a question that has become increasingly important to me: What if the extraordinary spiritual assistance he described was not merely a remarkable event in the past, but also a clue about possibilities awaiting us in the future?

I am not suggesting that everyone will become another Joseph Smith, or that his prophetic calling will somehow become commonplace.

I am wondering about something broader:

What if God is preparing to make extraordinary capabilities available to ordinary people in extraordinary abundance?

And what if this becomes one of the defining characteristics of the coming Zion?

I am choosing to believe this in the spirit of Alma 32. I do not claim to know precisely how or when these things will unfold. I am planting a seed of belief, giving it room to grow, and watching for its fruits.

And the possibilities fill me with hope.

I envision two overlapping futures: an early Zion, in which the beginnings of these developments become increasingly visible, and an eventual Zion, in which their consequences may surpass almost anything we presently imagine.

Three developments seem particularly important. The first is our growing understanding of how human beings develop sophisticated capabilities. The second is the emergence of technologies, especially artificial intellegence, that may allow us to construct environments optimized for acquiring those capabilities. The third—and by far the most consequential in my prediction—is an >extraordinary outpouring of the Spirit of God.

I suspect the first two may help prepare the way for the third. But I also suspect that the third may ultimately dwarf the first two.

We have always been WORLD builders

For years, I have been experimenting with a proposition that comes naturally to me as a sociologist: human beings are continually constructing and inhabiting worlds.

I am not referring primarily to physical worlds. I mean worlds of meaning, interpretation, expectation, memory, habit, relationship, and experience.

We live inside worlds constructed partly through language, stories, routines, symbols, social relationships, and the interpretations we place upon events. These worlds influence what we notice, what we believe is possible, how we respond to difficulties, and even what we experience as reality.

Most people participate in this process largely unwittingly. They inherit environments and learn to operate within them without giving much thought to the ways those environments are shaping them.

Others become increasingly conscious of the process. They recognize that they are world-builders and begin constructing their environments more deliberately.

I call this distinction witting and unwitting WORLD building .

My Jesus-Greg WORLD is an experiment in witting WORLD building. I am deliberately organizing elements of my daily life—my surroundings, music, rituals, work, imagination, and media—around remembering Jesus Christ.

I have a Jesus Pillow, a Jesus Shower, a Jesus Door, a Jesus Table, and many other reminders. I have redeemed songs, daily rehearsals, an ongoing work-opera, and Jesus TV, where I seek to observe my own life through the lens of discipleship.

The experiment is not primarily about producing religious artwork or creating an elaborate collection of reminders. It is about constructing an environment in which remembering Jesus becomes increasingly natural.

Instead of periodically interrupting my ordinary life to think about Jesus, I am attempting to make remembering Him part of the structure of ordinary life itself.

And I am beginning to see how this same principle could apply to an enormous range of human capabilities.

What if the WORLD could do much of the teaching?

Consider how people ordinarily acquire sophisticated skills.

My longtime friend Norm Wood spent years developing an extraordinarily integrative framework for understanding human effectiveness. His work occupied territory familiar to readers of Stephen Covey and other thinkers concerned with character, leadership, self-management, relationships, and human potential.

But Norm was casting a much wider net than any single author. He was gathering insights from many approaches and constructing conceptual maps that could guide people toward more effective ways of living.

I learned a great deal from Norm. I also discovered how difficult it could be to master such a comprehensive system.

My brother Steve provides another example of sophisticated human development. Over many years, he has practiced examining his perceptions and judgments before they produce emotional reactions. He compares this to a pain that seems to originate in his back but, when he pays closer attention, turns out to be coming from his stomach. Similarly, what first seems to call for anger may be better understood as hurt, worry, frustration, sorrow, or concern. His goal is not merely to suppress anger after it appears. It is to understand the situation so accurately that anger may never arise in the first place.

Steve says that, through sustained practice, this reconsideration can sometimes happen within a single breath. But his experience with his teenage daughter Heather reveals an equally important side of the skill.

During a difficult disagreement, Heather ran away. Steve found her, brought her home, and followed her into her bedroom after she slammed the door. While she lay on her bed, furious, he sat quietly on the floor with his back against the door. After about fifteen minutes she demanded, “WHAT?!” He merely shook his head. Ten minutes later she asked what he wanted. “I don't know,” he answered, truthfully.

He was not trying to control anger: he was not angry. He had recognized immediately that their father–daughter relationship was changing as Heather grew toward adulthood. What he could not yet see was how to proceed into what he later called an “un-experienced future” —a future neither of them had lived. When Heather asked again, more calmly, he explained his uncertainty about how parental authority and their relationship could continue. She cried and asked, “Can we go back the way it was?” Steve answered, “I can, what I don't know is, can you?” She said she could. They talked and hugged.

Years of practice had helped Steve avoid interpreting Heather's behavior as a personal injury demanding anger. Yet even with that skill, he needed a long, quiet interval to contemplate grief, responsibility, and the unfamiliar future of a relationship he loved.

Steve did not simply learn a better way to react to the world. He cultivated a different way to experience the world. That is an extraordinary achievement in its own right.

But what if the future offers another pathway to acquiring such competencies?

Imagine learning Spanish.

One approach is to attend classes, memorize vocabulary, study grammar, complete assignments, and practice speaking.

Another approach is to move to Mexico.

You find yourself surrounded by Spanish. Your neighbors speak Spanish. You buy groceries in Spanish. You encounter problems that require Spanish. Your friendships and daily activities continually expose you to the language.

Eighteen months later, you may have become remarkably proficient without having taken a single formal Spanish class.

You were learning all along. But much of the learning was embedded in the environment.

Now imagine extending that principle to sophisticated relationship skills.

What if a person could enter an environment carefully designed to cultivate patience, emotional understanding, conflict resolution, cooperation, humility, forgiveness, and compassion?

What if the person didn't need to spend years studying elaborate theories about these competencies?

What if the environment itself continually invited the person to practice them?

That is the educational possibility I find so exciting.

Instead of merely teaching people the lessons, we could construct WORLDS in which people live the lessons.

AI and the emergence of intelligent learning environments

Artificial intelligence introduces remarkable possibilities for this kind of WORLD building.

We are beginning to develop tools that can generate personalized stories, interactive simulations, adaptive learning experiences, virtual environments, and increasingly sophisticated forms of feedback.

Imagine taking Norm Wood's conceptual maps and using AI to translate their principles into everyday experiences.

Rather than requiring someone to master a complicated framework for perspective-taking, an AI-supported environment might repeatedly introduce situations that encourage the person to consider alternative interpretations.

Think of Steve and Heather. A thoughtful immersive environment might let a learner rehearse noticing the difference between feeling personally attacked and recognizing grief or concern about a changing relationship—while also learning that even excellent emotional judgment does not automatically tell us what to do next.

Rather than presenting a lengthy curriculum on cooperation, it might organize meaningful activities in which cooperation becomes an increasingly natural way to accomplish shared objectives.

Rather than merely explaining forgiveness, it might provide opportunities to rehearse difficult conversations, consider other people's circumstances, and develop more constructive responses.

Much of the instruction could become sublimated into the experience itself.

The participant would still be learning. But the learning might feel less like attending school and more like living.

Of course, these environments would need to respect agency. People should understand their general purposes, choose to participate, and retain the ability to question what they are being taught. An environment designed to cultivate wisdom should not become a sophisticated instrument of manipulation.

And AI-generated experiences cannot replace real human relationships. The ultimate test of relationship skills is what happens when we encounter actual people with their own needs, choices, and perspectives.

Nevertheless, I believe we are approaching a period when the ability to construct personalized learning environments will become increasingly accessible.

What once required large institutions, specialized educators, and enormous resources may become possible for individuals, families, and small communities.

And this brings me back to Zion.

What kind of people does Zion require?

Zion is not merely a place where people possess impressive abilities.

In Moses 7:18, Zion is described as a people of one heart and one mind, dwelling in righteousness, with no poor among them.

Consider the sophistication of the human capabilities implied by that description.

Imagine the emotional maturity required to sustain a community of one heart and one mind.

Imagine the interpersonal understanding, trustworthiness, humility, patience, cooperation, generosity, and willingness to sacrifice.

Imagine the ability to recognize and resolve conflicts before they become destructive.

Imagine the wisdom required to organize a society in which people genuinely care for one another.

These are not trivial competencies.

Many of the greatest thinkers in psychology, sociology, leadership, organizational development, and personal effectiveness have devoted their careers to understanding fragments of these challenges.

And yet Zion envisions an entire community characterized by extraordinary relational unity.

Sharon Eubank's teachings about covenants, service, trust, and community have helped me think about this in a new way.

Perhaps the deepest purpose of sophisticated human-effectiveness skills is not simply to make individuals more productive or successful.

Perhaps their greatest purpose is to help people become capable of living together in love.

But this raises a question.

Must every inhabitant of Zion become an expert in psychology, leadership, emotional intelligence, and conflict resolution?

Must everyone study Norm's maps?

Must everyone undergo years of deliberate training comparable to Steve's?

Perhaps some will. Perhaps many will benefit enormously from such instruction.

But I am beginning to believe that the Lord has other possibilities available to Him.

And some of those possibilities may be miraculous.

The third pathway: Gifts of the Spirit

Return to the example of learning Spanish.

We have considered two pathways: formal education and immersion.

But the scriptures introduce a third possibility.

The gift of tongues.

According to Latter-day Saint belief, God can grant gifts that enable people to accomplish things beyond their ordinary preparation.

The gift of tongues offers a particularly vivid example.

A person might acquire Spanish through years of study. Another might acquire it through immersion in Mexico.

But what if God chose to grant someone the ability to communicate in sophisticated Spanish without either pathway?

That would not merely be a more efficient educational program.

It would be a different kind of event.

A gift.

Now consider the implications for the other gifts of the Spirit.

The scriptures speak of wisdom, knowledge, faith, healing, discernment, prophecy, and other spiritual gifts.

What if the Lord chose to make such gifts available in exceptional abundance?

What if extraordinary interpersonal understanding could sometimes be received through spiritual inspiration rather than acquired exclusively through years of training?

What if someone could perceive the needs of another person with remarkable clarity?

What if a person facing an extraordinarily difficult conflict could receive wisdom beyond what their ordinary experience would suggest?

What if people could be endowed with exceptional capacities for teaching, comforting, reconciling, leading, and loving?

These possibilities are not equivalent to a guarantee that God will instantly provide every skill someone desires. Nor would a claimed spiritual impression eliminate the need for humility, verification, or accountability.

But I believe the scriptural doctrine of spiritual gifts opens a much larger horizon than conventional educational theory can encompass.

And I suspect that horizon may be especially relevant to the coming Zion.

Miracles beyond ordinary preparation

The scriptures and Latter-day Saint history contain accounts of people accomplishing things beyond what their ordinary preparation would seem to explain.

David confronted Goliath with faith in God.

Joseph Smith testified that he translated the Book of Mormon through the gift and power of God, rather than through conventional education in ancient languages.

President Russell M. Nelson has described experiences of divine guidance in his work as a surgeon. Importantly, these accounts involve a man who had already acquired extraordinary medical expertise. His experience illustrates the possibility of inspired assistance working alongside disciplined preparation.

I also find myself borrowing images from popular culture.

In Star Wars , Luke Skywalker is urged to trust the Force rather than rely exclusively upon ordinary calculation.

In The Matrix , Trinity requests the knowledge needed to fly a helicopter, and the necessary capability becomes available almost instantaneously.

These are fictional illustrations, not theological evidence. But they help me imagine a question that I believe is worth considering:

What if God can make sophisticated capabilities available to His children in ways that do not depend entirely upon the ordinary sequence of instruction, practice, and mastery?

And what if, in the future, He chooses to do so far more frequently?

That possibility changes how I imagine Zion.

A whisper from the dust: Mormon, Moroni, and the gifts of God

Not merely preserved for our day. Written with us in mind.

They knew what it looked like when a society began to come apart.

And they wrote for a future audience.

Moroni even declared that Jesus Christ had shown him our day, and that he knew our doing (Mormon 8:35).

Think about that.

These men witnessed a civilization approaching destruction, and they addressed another civilization that would face its own profound spiritual and social challenges.

They knew something about the dangers awaiting us.

And what counsel did they leave?

The gifts of God.

He speaks of wisdom, knowledge, faith, healing, miracles, prophecy, the beholding of angels and ministering spirits, tongues, and the interpretation of tongues.

And then he warns us:

"And I would exhort you, my beloved brethren, that ye remember that every good gift cometh of Christ." — Moroni 10:18

He also warns against denying the gifts of God, teaching that they come according to faith and the will of God.

Now, I do not claim that Moroni was secretly describing artificial intelligence, immersive learning environments, or the particular future I am imagining.

Here is a prophet who has witnessed the destruction of his people, who has seen our day in vision, and who is writing his final words to future generations.

And near the conclusion of his record, he directs our attention toward extraordinary gifts from God.

Not merely toward becoming more intellectually accomplished.

Not merely toward becoming more technologically sophisticated.

But toward remembering and receiving the gifts of God.

And this is where I find myself making a sacred guess.

What if Mormon and Moroni understood something about the extraordinary spiritual resources that would be needed in the latter days?

What if their counsel about spiritual gifts is especially consequential for the building of Zion?

I cannot establish that this is precisely what Moroni intended. But I can choose to explore the possibility, remembering that Moroni's concluding invitation is ultimately to come unto Christ and be perfected in Him.

And I can almost imagine an ancient voice speaking across the centuries.

When the windows of secular heaven open

We seem to be standing at the brink of an extraordinary technological transformation.

Artificial intelligence. Virtual reality. Robotics. Intelligent assistants. Increasingly sophisticated systems capable of performing tasks that once required years of specialized education.

The news is filled with these developments. Publications like Fast Company , along with countless technology researchers, business leaders, and commentators, continually describe the possibilities and disruptions that may lie ahead.

We do not know exactly how quickly these technologies will advance, or which predictions will prove correct.

But we know that something consequential is happening.

And we can already see how ordinary people encounter these developments.

Most of us did not invent artificial intelligence.

We did not spend decades developing the mathematics, building the computing infrastructure, or training the models.

We simply discovered that ChatGPT, Claude, and other remarkable tools had become available.

And we began using them.

Some of us approached them with trepidation. Others with enthusiasm. Many with a mixture of both.

But consider how familiar this pattern is.

Our forefathers did not individually invent the automobile. They did not personally design the internal combustion engine, establish the factories, or construct the national highway systems.

For most people, the great technological achievement of their generation eventually became something they could simply purchase and use.

They bought the car.

And their world changed.

Likewise, ordinary families did not invent television, build rockets, or develop the electrical and communications infrastructure that transformed modern civilization.

Yet they became beneficiaries of those developments.

Technological capabilities that once seemed miraculous became ordinary features of everyday life.

It is almost as though the windows of a kind of secular heaven were opened, and astonishing environmental blessings began pouring down upon our homes and lives.

Not that these inventions were literally revelations or that every technological development was good.

But consider the experience of receiving capabilities that we did not personally create.

We wake up one day, and something extraordinary has become available.

We learn to use it.

And the environment in which we live changes.

We did not have to become the inventors of the blessings in order to become their beneficiaries.

Now imagine the next several decades.

What if sophisticated AI systems, immersive environments, robots, and other technologies make capabilities available to ordinary people that today belong primarily to highly trained specialists?

What if the tools themselves increasingly perform portions of the work that previously required extraordinary individual expertise?

And what if the environments surrounding us become so capable that many forms of learning, creating, communicating, and accomplishing become radically easier?

I see this as a kind of secular approximation of the pattern described in Joel.

Not the fulfillment of Joel's prophecy.

Not the equivalent of the Holy Ghost.

But an intriguing parallel.

Extraordinary capabilities becoming widely available, descending into ordinary human life, transforming what people can do without requiring each recipient to have personally developed the underlying capability.

And this is where my sacred guess becomes especially exciting.

Early Zion and eventual Zion

Human beings begin constructing increasingly sophisticated environments for learning and development.

AI and other technologies make many of these environments more accessible.

People become increasingly conscious of the ways their surroundings shape their thoughts, habits, relationships, and spiritual lives.

Some begin deliberately constructing Christ-centered WORLDS.

But eventual Zion, as I imagine it, involves something greater.

Some through immersion.

Some through long practice in loving communities.

They may complement and reinforce one another.

The Book of Mormon: A Shadow and Type of the Coming Zion

The question I raised at the beginning of this essay now returns with greater force: What if Joseph Smith was not merely an exception, but a preview?

I am beginning to see the coming forth of the Book of Mormon as a possible shadow and type of the coming Zion.

Consider what happened. A young farm boy, lacking the formal education normally required to translate an ancient record, testified that he brought forth the Book of Mormon by the gift and power of God. The plates had a history on earth, and Joseph still had to act, dictate, persevere, and exercise faith. Yet, according to his testimony, the extraordinary enabling power came from Heaven.

In that sense, the Book of Mormon flowed from Heaven—not as a finished book dropping out of the sky, but as a work made possible through divine gifts that exceeded ordinary human preparation.

And what became of that gift? Something given through the experience of one ordinary young man became a book available to millions.

Then comes a remarkable detail. The Book of Mormon, whose translation Joseph attributed to the gift and power of God, closes with Moroni inviting its readers to remember the gifts of God and not deny them (Moroni 10:8–18, 24).

The book came forth through what Joseph described as an extraordinary divine gift—and it calls its readers to believe in the reality of divine gifts.

What if that is more than an inspiring historical coincidence? What if it is a pattern worth pondering?

I do not mean that every person will become another Joseph Smith, or that his prophetic calling will be repeated. Nor do I claim that scripture guarantees the timing or scale of the future I imagine. This is my sacred guess, not an official interpretation or a timetable.

But what if the Lord intends to pour out extraordinary capacities for wisdom, understanding, discernment, cooperation, and love upon ordinary people in an abundance we have scarcely seen?

What if the coming forth of the Book of Mormon was not only the beginning of the Restoration, but also a preview of how God intends to build Zion?

Heaven supplies what human preparation alone cannot. Human beings receive, participate, and act.

And perhaps some of the greatest miracles of the coming Zion will be found not merely in what people know, but in how extraordinarily they learn to love.

Joseph Smith and the Work Already Done

I think again of Joseph Smith.

The Book of Mormon did not begin with Joseph.

Long before he was born, prophets had lived, preached, written, compiled, abridged, preserved, and entrusted their sacred record to the future.

Imagine the accumulated labor.

Thousands upon thousands of hours. Generations of preparation. Lives devoted to a work whose eventual beneficiaries would never meet the people who performed it.

Then came Joseph.

A young farm boy who testified that he translated the record by the gift and power of God.

Joseph still had work to do. He had to respond, dictate, persevere, and participate in bringing the book forth. His experience was not effortless.

But he did not have to become Mormon or Moroni. He did not have to reproduce their lifetimes of labor.

He received an inheritance of sacred work already accomplished, and, according to his testimony, extraordinary divine assistance in bringing that inheritance into the world.

The preparation was immense. The gift was extraordinary. And the recipient was invited to participate in something far larger than himself.

What if this is a shadow and type of something awaiting Zion?

The ChatGPT Comparison

I did not invent artificial intelligence.

I did not develop the mathematics, manufacture the processors, build the data centers, or spend decades refining the technologies that eventually made ChatGPT available.

Others did that work.

An astonishing amount of it.

And then, one day, I began using the result.

I received access to capabilities that would have been inconceivable to me only a short time earlier.

The analogy is imperfect, of course. Artificial intelligence is a human technology, with human limitations. Spiritual gifts, as I understand them, are received according to God's will and cannot be purchased, programmed, or summoned on demand.

But the comparison helps me imagine something.

What if the coming Zion involves extraordinary blessings whose preparation is already far beyond anything I can personally comprehend?

What if I have been assuming that I must somehow become capable of accomplishing the entire work, when my actual assignment may be to receive an inheritance and learn to inhabit it?

Not to manufacture Zion.

Not to command Heaven.

Not to pretend that difficult things require no effort.

The Strength to Rest

Here is where Victory Hall becomes especially important to me.

Learning. Planning. Working. Building. Striving. Attempting to become capable of doing what I could not previously do.

There is much good in that.

But I wonder whether another kind of strength is becoming increasingly important.

The strength to stop assuming that everything depends upon my own capacity.

The strength to trust.

The strength to wait without surrendering hope.

The strength to recognize an offered gift.

The strength to receive without resisting.

The strength to inhabit what is already available.

And the strength to discern what is genuinely being offered, rather than insisting that God fulfill every expectation I have imagined.

Perhaps receiving an inheritance requires a different kind of courage than earning a possession.

I am reminded of the language of endowment.

To be endowed is to be given something.

And what if some of the most consequential things awaiting God's children are not achievements we manufacture, but capacities with which He chooses to endow us?

Wisdom.

Discernment.

Understanding.

Love.

The ability to cooperate.

The ability to build Zion.

Perhaps these gifts will often develop gradually, through long experience and faithful practice.

But perhaps, at times, God will also give extraordinary assistance beyond what ordinary preparation could explain.

That is the possibility I am choosing to believe.

Prophecy TV: Rehearsing the future now

This brings me back to my own experiment.

I increasingly sense that Jesus wants me to look toward the future with hope and to begin rehearsing aspects of that future in my own life.

I call this Prophecy TV.

It is my way of imagining what may be coming and experimenting with what I can begin doing now.

I do not need to wait for sophisticated virtual reality systems or artificial intelligence capable of constructing entire interactive worlds.

I can begin with a pillow.

A door.

A song.

A meal.

A conversation.

An hour of work.

I can deliberately arrange these ordinary elements of my life to invite remembrance of Jesus.

I can seek to become more attentive to the Spirit.

I can observe whether this way of living makes me more patient, more charitable, more forgiving, more discerning, and more willing to serve.

And I can continue learning from people like Norm, Steve, and Sharon Eubank, whose experiences illuminate different aspects of human development.

My Jesus-Greg WORLD is not Zion. It is a personal experiment, with all the limitations and uncertainties that entails.

But perhaps it is a small rehearsal of something larger.

Perhaps future generations will possess extraordinary tools for constructing environments in which learning and spiritual formation become deeply integrated.

And perhaps God will bless those generations with gifts that exceed anything their tools could produce.

I find that possibility exhilarating.

The experiment continues

There is an important distinction between predicting something, choosing to believe it, and knowing that it will occur.

I am choosing to believe.

I am not claiming to possess a complete timetable of the future. Nor am I claiming that every hopeful image produced by my sacred imagination constitutes revelation.

I am following Alma's invitation to experiment upon the word.

I am giving this hope a place in my life.

And I intend to examine its fruits.

If my experiment produces more talk about Jesus but less love for my neighbors, something is wrong.

If it produces grand expectations about future miracles but diminishes my willingness to serve in the present, something is wrong.

But if it helps me become more charitable, more hopeful, more responsive to the needs of others, and more willing to seek God's guidance, those are fruits worth cultivating.

The great hope of Zion is not merely that human beings will acquire astonishing abilities.

It is that they will become astonishingly good at loving one another.

Perhaps AI will help.

Perhaps immersive environments will help.

Perhaps the great maps of human effectiveness will help.

I believe they can.

But my greatest hope is that God will do something far beyond what our best technologies and educational systems could accomplish.

That He will pour out His Spirit in extraordinary abundance.

That people will receive gifts they could never have manufactured for themselves.

That sophisticated capacities for wisdom, understanding, cooperation, and love will become increasingly available.

And that the world will witness something marvelous: a people whose extraordinary capabilities are matched by extraordinary charity.

This is the future I am choosing to imagine.

This is the seed I am choosing to plant.

And this is why I am so hopeful about Zion.

Learn what you can learn. Live what you can live. Receive with gratitude whatever God may give.

And above all:

GO BIG ON JESUS.

For perhaps the greatest educational breakthrough of the coming Zion will not be something humanity invents.
