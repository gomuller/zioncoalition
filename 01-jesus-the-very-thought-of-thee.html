JESUS, THE VERY THOUGHT OF THEE

The First Thought · The Jesus Pillow · The Singularity of Thought

The MORE Jesus = More Reality Experiment — By Saint Greg

My daily opera begins with the deliberate selection of a thought. Jesus. Before the world's competing invitations occupy my attention, I feed my mind its first organizing thought.

Why? Because Jesus wants me to, I guess. I believe. I sacredly imagine. I wager. And then I act upon that wager.

The opening song of the Jesus Pillow

1. Jesus, the very thought of thee
With sweetness fills my breast;
But sweeter far thy face to see
And in thy presence rest.

2. Nor voice can sing, nor heart can frame,
Nor can the mem’ry find
A sweeter sound than thy blest name,
O Savior of mankind!

3. O hope of ev’ry contrite heart,
O joy of all the meek,
To those who fall, how kind thou art!
How good to those who seek!

4. Jesus, our only joy be thou,
As thou our prize wilt be;
Jesus, be thou our glory now,
And thru eternity.

1. Kabat-Zinn meets the Jesus Pillow

Jon Kabat-Zinn teaches the practice of deliberately directing attention, noticing when it wanders, and returning. I am taking that attentional discipline into an explicitly Christ-centered experiment.

Kabat-Zinn might say: Return to the breath. My Jesus Pillow says: Return to Jesus.

And I don't necessarily abandon the breath. The breath, pillow, bed, morning, songs, and eventually the whole day become occasions for returning to Jesus. The pillow becomes a kind of Liahona—not because it is inherently miraculous, but because I've made it a recurring instrument of orientation.

2. The first verse contains an entire spiritual trajectory

THOUGHT: Jesus enters attention. The intentional first thought.

SWEETNESS: Thought becomes affection. Attention begins shaping desire.

SEEING: Affection seeks encounter. Remembering Jesus becomes a longing to see Him.

REST: Encounter culminates in presence. Not merely thinking about Jesus, but resting with Him.

The hymn says the thought of Jesus is sweet, but His presence is sweeter. The thought is not the destination. It is the doorway. My daily opera begins at that doorway.

3. The four verses expand the singularity

Verse 1: From thought to presence.

Verse 2: From thought to name, sound, and memory.

Verse 3: From personal sweetness to kindness, mercy, and seeking.

Verse 4: From this morning to eternity.

One thought, repeatedly chosen, becomes an organizing principle for a life.

Here is my proposed singularity of thought: not a mind reduced to thinking only about Jesus, but a mind increasingly capable of discovering relationships between Jesus and everything else. That is a much more expansive singularity.

4. And then there's the neuroscience

Kabat-Zinn's teaching suggests an important distinction: the content of attention and the practice of attending are not the same thing. Research on attention, learning, and neuroplasticity gives us reason to think repeated mental practices can influence habits of perception and response. But whether a particular devotional practice produces a specific neurological change—or an encounter with God—requires separate evidence.

My wager doesn't have to pretend otherwise.

I am deliberately feeding my mind Jesus first, because I believe that what I repeatedly attend to will increasingly shape what I notice, what I love, what I remember, and how I live.

And then I watch what happens. That is the experimental character of this project.

5. The most beautiful paradox

I call it the Jesus Pillow. A pillow is where I surrender the effort of holding myself upright. It is where I rest. And my first song begins by inviting me into the sweetness of a thought, then immediately tells me something sweeter awaits beyond the thought: presence, rest.

My singularity is not simply an increasingly powerful thought machine. It is a person learning to surrender more and more of his attention to a relationship he believes is already available.

I begin my day with one thought, not because I want to think fewer thoughts, but because I want all the other thoughts to have somewhere beautiful to go.

And then there is Moroni 4:3: the covenant to always remember Him. My first morning thought is a small, deliberate approximation of that enormous promise. I cannot consciously think about Jesus every second. But perhaps I can increasingly organize my waking life so that more and more ordinary moments become invitations to remember Him.

The first thought is Jesus. The experiment is what happens to all the thoughts that follow.
