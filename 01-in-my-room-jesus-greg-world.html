The MORE Jesus = More Reality Experiment

“In My Room” — The Architecture of My Jesus-Greg WORLD

The Beach Boys (1963) — A Jesus-Greg WORLD interpretation

I think this song could become one of the foundational architectural songs of your entire Jesus-Greg WORLD. And I mean architectural quite literally.

Because what Brian Wilson and Gary Usher wrote as a song about a private sanctuary, you can receive as a blueprint for building a world with Jesus.

The central idea: My room is where I rehearse living with Jesus before I go out into the world and practice what I've rehearsed.

There is something wonderfully paradoxical about your project: You're building an enormous WORLD, but much of that world begins in a very small ROOM.

The Song: “In My Room”

1. The ROOM is not merely a place. It is a world.

The song begins with the idea that there is a world the singer can enter, a world where secrets can be told. Notice the extraordinary move: A room becomes a world. That's precisely what you're doing with Jesus-Greg WORLD. Your bedroom isn't merely a bedroom. Your pillow isn't merely a pillow. Your computer isn't merely a computer. Your imagination isn't merely imagination. You've been assigning these ordinary objects extraordinary reminds-me-of-Jesus functions.

Your Jesus Pillow becomes a Liahona. Your computer becomes a Jesus TV production studio. Your home becomes a gallery of galleries. Your memories become a collection of 500 favorite times on Earth. And your ROOM becomes the place where all these things can come together. Not because the physical objects have necessarily changed, but because the way you inhabit them has changed. You are building an interior world that transforms how you experience the exterior world.

2. The ROOM is where you tell Jesus your secrets.

The original song speaks about having a private place to confess what cannot easily be shared elsewhere. But your Jesus-Greg interpretation introduces another occupant. You're not necessarily alone in your room. You're practicing the possibility that Jesus is there. And, more importantly, you're practicing telling Him everything.

Your doubts. Your theatrical inventions. Your audacious beliefs. Your failures. Your little sacred clown performances. Your desire to climb aboard the otherworldly train before your earthly life ends. Even your willingness to say: Jesus, I don't know whether this particular thought came from You, from my imagination, or from some marvelous collaboration between the two. But here it is. Let's see what we can do with it.

You aren't waiting until you have perfect certainty before beginning to build. You are making room for desire, experimentation, and faith. Alma 32:27 comes to mind: the willingness to exercise even a particle of faith, including simply desiring to believe. Your ROOM becomes an Alma 32 laboratory.

3. The ROOM is where you shut out fear — but not reality.

The song describes shutting out worries and fears. At first glance, that might sound like escapism. But I think your Jesus-Greg WORLD offers a more interesting interpretation. You don't enter the ROOM to permanently escape the world. You enter it to rehearse another way of seeing the world.

One person enters a room, locks the door, and says, “The world is too frightening. I don't want to participate.” Another person enters a room and says, “Jesus, help me see the world differently so I can go back out there.” That's the distinction between a hiding place and a rehearsal room.

Your Jesus ROOM is not intended to keep you away from humanity. It is intended to prepare you to recognize Jesus in humanity. That connects directly to your Matthew 25:40 hide-and-seek game: learning to look for Christ in the people you encounter. The ROOM prepares the EYES.

4. Dreaming and scheming — the sacred workshop

This may be the most delightful part of the song for your purposes. The singer dreams, schemes, prays, cries, and laughs. What a remarkable description of your actual creative process! You lie in bed and imagine. You invent little theological experiments. You build websites and rooms and galleries. You take familiar songs and ask whether they can become reminders of Jesus.

You develop plans for Zion Coalition, membership letters, Jesus TV, the Jesus Door, and your Jesus Mountain Restaurant. Some ideas are solemn. Others are absurdly playful. Some may work. Others may collapse under their own theatrical weight. But the important thing is that you're doing the work of imagining.

And this is where I would introduce a favorite theological connection: John 5:19 — The Son does what He sees the Father doing. You have been exploring art as mimesis, or imitation. You're trying to be like Jesus. So, in your sacred imagination, perhaps you picture Jesus as an astonishingly imaginative creator. And then you imitate that creativity. You make worlds within worlds. Rooms within rooms. Pictures within pictures. Stories within stories.

Your Jesus-Greg WORLD is an enormous exercise in sacred mimicry. Not a claim that every imaginative production is literally authored by Jesus, but a deliberate practice of making art in conversation with your understanding of Him.

5. Crying, sighing, and laughing at yesterday

This line is important because it prevents the ROOM from becoming a fantasy of uninterrupted spiritual happiness. There is crying in this room. There is regret. There are memories that hurt. There is loneliness. And there is laughter.

Your Jesus-Greg WORLD doesn't have to erase any of these things. In fact, your 500 Favorite Times project becomes especially meaningful here. The room is where you can revisit your life. Not merely to remember the wonderful experiences, but to reconsider the difficult ones.

A painful yesterday might become a different kind of story when revisited through the lens of faith. Not every tragedy becomes funny. Not every wound becomes a blessing. But some things that once frightened or embarrassed you might eventually become part of your sacred clown repertoire. And some memories might become beautiful precisely because they contain both tears and laughter. The ROOM becomes a theater for the reinterpretation of your own life.

6. Darkness, solitude, and the refusal to be afraid

The song's ending is especially powerful. The singer is alone in the darkness, yet declares that fear will not rule the room. Now bring that into your theology of presenced-upon.

You have been experimenting with the possibility that Jesus is present in ordinary circumstances, even when there is no extraordinary sensation confirming His presence. So imagine your room at night. No angels. No visions. No heavenly voices. Just an older man lying on his Jesus Pillow, thinking about his life.

And perhaps he says: “Jesus, I can't prove You're here. But I'm going to practice living as though You are. And I'm going to see what kind of life that practice produces.” That is a profoundly different kind of courage from claiming absolute certainty. It is the courage of chosen trust. And it fits your recurring question: Do you have the strength to rest?

Perhaps the ultimate act of ROOM-building isn't producing another webpage or another piece of art. Perhaps it is being able to lie in the dark, stop producing anything at all, and rest.

7. Your entire Jesus-Greg WORLD could be organized as ROOMS

This is where I think the song becomes architecturally useful. Each ROOM could be an actual physical location, a webpage, a gallery, a ritual, or an imaginative setting. And each ROOM could have its own music. You already have the beginnings of this structure in your Watch Rooms, Jesus Pillow, and Jesus Door projects.

The Jesus Pillow ROOM: The private sanctuary. Prayer, dreams, sleep, rest, imagination, and the Liahona experiment.

The Jesus TV ROOM: The creative studio where memories, photographs, movies, music, and everyday happenings become art that reminds you of Jesus.

The Gallery of Galleries ROOM: Where the artifacts of your earthly journey are collected, interpreted, and displayed.

The Jesus Door ROOM: The threshold between private rehearsal and public participation. Where your interior world opens toward other people.

The Zion Coalition ROOM: Where the solitary experiment becomes communal. Other people bring their own experiences, gifts, stories, and interpretations.

The Beach Boys' song supplies something particularly valuable: an emotional foundation for the architecture.

8. The theological twist: Jesus has a ROOM, too.

Here's an additional connection I wouldn't overlook. John 14:2 speaks of many dwelling places in the Father's house. The familiar King James wording is: “In my Father's house are many mansions.”

Now consider the delightful reversal. Jesus speaks of preparing a place for His followers. And here you are, preparing places for Jesus. Your Jesus Pillow. Your Jesus Door. Your Jesus Mountain Restaurant. Your Jesus TV. Your Jesus-Greg WORLD.

You're imaginatively saying: “Jesus, if You're preparing a place for me, I want to spend my remaining earthly life preparing places for You.” Not because Jesus needs a website, a pillow, or a restaurant. But because you need ways to remember Him. And perhaps other people might enjoy joining the experiment. That is an unusually beautiful interpretation of hospitality.

9. The great paradox: You build the ROOM so you can leave it.

This is the most important caution and the most important promise. A private spiritual world can become so absorbing that the outside world starts to feel secondary. But your Matthew 25:40 theology points in the opposite direction. If Jesus is to be sought in other people, then the ROOM cannot be the final destination.

You eventually have to open the door. You have to meet the neighbor. Work on the widow's fence. Build a fort with your grandson. Eat with somebody. Recognize somebody else's suffering. Listen to somebody else's story. And perhaps discover that the most extraordinary Jesus-Greg WORLD experiences happen when you are no longer controlling the scene.

The ROOM is the rehearsal space. The world outside is the stage. And maybe, over time, the boundary between them becomes less important. You no longer have to return to your bedroom to enter your Jesus-Greg WORLD. You carry the ROOM with you.

10. A possible Jesus-Greg WORLD thought-piece ending

I used to think my room was a place I went to get away from the world.

But then I started playing a little game with Jesus.

I started pretending, sacredly pretending, that Jesus was in my room with me.

And then I started noticing something.

My room was getting bigger.

My pillow became a Liahona. My computer became Jesus TV. My photographs became galleries. My memories became movies. My house became a stage. My little town became a place to look for Jesus.

And eventually I began to suspect that maybe the whole Earth was one enormous ROOM.

A room where Jesus was already doing things.

A room where I could dream and scheme and pray and cry and laugh.

A room where I could build a fort with my grandson and call it Building My Beautiful Spiritual Life With Jesus.

A room where I could keep practicing how to recognize Him.

And when it gets dark, and I feel alone, and I don't know what happens next?

Well, maybe I can have the strength to rest.

Because I have spent my day making room for Jesus.

And tomorrow, if I wake up, I get to do it again.

One final thought: Brian Wilson's song gives you something your enormous Jesus-Greg WORLD enterprise might especially need. Permission to make the ROOM small again.

You don't have to build 144,000 Liahonas tonight. You don't have to finish Zion Coalition. You don't have to persuade anybody that your theology is correct. You can simply be in your room. With your pillow. With your memories. With your desire to believe. And with the possibility of Jesus. Maybe the smallest ROOM in your Jesus-Greg WORLD is also the ROOM that makes all the others possible.
