JESUS-GREG WORLD · NOTES FROM THE ARTIST · 2026

Let the Art Do the Preaching

What Jonathan helped me notice about the FRONT STAGE.

Something changed in 2026. I began sensing that Jesus was moving me toward a different kind of FRONT STAGE: more art, fewer explanations. Less Greg standing beside the picture explaining what it means. More picture. More invitation. More room for the viewer to encounter something without my commentary arriving first.

I have spent years trying to say what I think Jesus is doing in my life. Sometimes I produce a thousand words when a few might suffice. And I still love the thousand words. They belong BACK STAGE, where curious people can wander among the footnotes, machinery, experiments, and improbable theological connections. But increasingly I suspect that the FRONT STAGE may need to breathe.

Then Jonathan wrote to me about a sacrament artwork. The picture connected belief in forgiveness through the sacrament with the curious way we speak of owning a home even when a bank holds a mortgage. It also carried this invitation: “Technical points are overrated: go believe wild things!” Jonathan said the art reached him. He could feel himself being motivated to believe. His heart was working on it.

And then came the surprise. I had offered him a thousand words of explanation. He declined. Reading more, he sensed, might actually detract from what he was feeling and thinking. Instead, he asked for more art. His heart yearned for it.

That response deserves my attention. Jonathan was not asking me to prove a doctrine, or declaring that every analogy is airtight. He was reporting an experience: a small, surprising connection had opened a door in him. An explanation might have rushed through that door, rearranged the furniture, and crowded out the guest.

Perhaps art sometimes works precisely because it does not finish the thought for us. A metaphor holds two unlikely things together long enough for us to notice something new. A picture can make a proposition feel imaginable before we have decided what we think about it. The viewer becomes a participant, not merely a recipient of Greg’s conclusions.

That does not mean technical points are worthless. Mortgages and sacramental covenants are not identical, and an image is not a substitute for careful thinking. Wild belief can be mistaken. But careful thinking has its place and its time. There are moments when analysis clarifies; there are other moments when premature analysis interrupts attention, wonder, or the first fragile stirrings of faith.

I think of Jesus teaching in parables, offering images that listeners could carry away and ponder. Not every seed was immediately explained. Not every door was opened for every audience at the same moment. A story could keep working after the storyteller stopped speaking.

So perhaps this is one reason I feel drawn toward a more art-centered FRONT STAGE in 2026. I can make the work, offer it, and resist the urge to supervise the encounter. The thousand words can wait behind another door. Jonathan has reminded me that what matters may be happening quietly in somebody else’s heart.

More art, then. Less explaining it before it has had a chance to speak. And if someone asks what it means, there is always BACK STAGE.
