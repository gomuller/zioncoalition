THE MORE JESUS = MORE REALITY EXPERIMENT

The Investigator Was Under Construction

The One-Week Menu, Norm Wood, Jesus Groundhog Day, and the gifts through which we may bear one another's burdens.

By Saint Greg — Wake-Up-with-Jesus reflections, November 4, 2026

What if the things I was drawn to investigate were not merely the subjects of my education, but also the instruments of it? And what if those instruments help us bear one another's burdens?

1. The Investigator Was Under Construction

I woke this morning in my Wake-Up-with-Jesus scene with an unexpected convergence of old puzzle pieces. I understand the experience as Jesus inviting me to see a pattern in the way I have been drawn, over decades, to investigate the ordinary and search for its hidden first principles.

What if the things I have been drawn to investigate throughout my life were not merely the subjects of my education, but also the instruments of it? What if, while I investigated the One-Week Menu, God was also developing the investigator who would someday turn those same capacities toward investigating Him?

2. Jesus Groundhog Day: The Wheels Go Round and Round

I geeked out for a decade-plus over something that sounds almost comically domestic: the recurring meals of human existence. The wheels go round and round. People eat breakfast, lunch, and dinner, then do it again. They say they could never eat the same things every week, while often doing approximately that already.

There it was: an underlying One-Week Menu approximation, hiding in plain sight. I did not invent recurrence. I recognized it. I named it. I began asking how to make it better. Survey the many. Choose the few. Inhabit the one.

3. The Easy Discovery and the Hard Tradeoffs

The conceptual breakthrough was almost embarrassingly simple. But optimizing the system was a lifetime-sized problem. Taste, ease of preparation, cost, nutritional density, correlation of ingredients, shopping, storage, cleanup, and the preferences of other people in my home all tugged in different directions.

The menu was not merely a list of recipes. It was an investigation into approximations, competing goods, and what happens when a person deliberately improves a recurring system. A family could inherit the principles without copying every meal. That was the OneHome ambition: all we have, why we have it, how to copy and customize it, and how to teach others.

4. Norm Wood and the Gifts of Human Difference

Then there were the years with Dr. Norm Wood. Norm could geek out on human functioning as relentlessly as I could geek out on a menu. Personality, gifts, weaknesses, measurement, dispositions, practices, the expanding theory of self: we spent years exploring that territory.

I now wonder whether those investigations were another part of the preparation. Norm helped me look for underlying patterns in human beings, just as the menu taught me to look for patterns in meals. Perhaps God was honing an instrument through repeated use, even before I understood what I would eventually use it to investigate.

5. 2015: Another Story Must Begin

In 2015, my investigative appetite turned with increasing intensity toward the Story of Jesus. Who is this Jesus? How does a personal relationship with an invisible Redeemer work? Is there an underlying structure, a recurring approximation, a spiritual menu hiding in plain sight?

The subject changed. The machinery of inquiry did not. I wanted to get MORE Jesus, and I wanted to get to the heart of the matter. Where is the E = mc²? I know you are hiding there! And this morning the pieces seemed to fit: perhaps I was being drawn all along. Drawn. Wrought upon. Drawn again.

6. Is the Desire Itself a Gift?

Here is the important distinction. Perhaps God did not merely find a convenient later use for a personality I happened to possess. Perhaps the desire to investigate—the appetite, the persistence, the delight in first principles—was itself among the ways He wrought upon me.

I cannot demonstrate that this is what God intended. I can say that, as I pondered this morning and tried to #HearHim, the interpretation illuminated decades of my life. The investigator himself may have been under construction while he thought he was constructing menus and theories.

7. Moroni 10, Myers-Briggs, and the Many Gifts of God

And if this is a possible way God works upon me, why would I assume He works upon everybody through the same psychological doorway? Moroni 10 and 1 Corinthians 12 speak of diverse gifts. What if our theology of gifts has been too narrow?

One person is drawn to coherence, another to harmony, another to music, comedy, invention, service, craftsmanship, storytelling, or beauty. Personality frameworks such as Myers-Briggs offer one vocabulary for considering differences, although fixed MBTI types are not established scientific categories. The broader reality of differing temperaments, interests, and abilities is unmistakable.

Perhaps God works with and through those differences. Perhaps He even guides development through circumstances and experience. Questions about DNA, prenatal life, and childhood environments are more speculative, and they bring difficult questions about suffering and freedom. I do not need to settle them to begin exploring the possibility.

8. Axl Rose and the Unwitting Builders of Zion

What about Axl Rose? What about the millions of other Axl Roses, people whose remarkable gifts do not arrive wrapped in conventional religious language? What if some are being wrought upon, and contributing something to the eventual building of Zion, wittingly or unwittingly?

That does not mean every expression of a gift is good, or that every impulse is divine. A gift can be real without every use of it being righteous. But it may mean that the body of Christ is larger, stranger, and more various in its preparatory work than our usual categories allow.

Perhaps people are drawn toward different parts of the elephant. And perhaps they are also given different ways of touching the same part. A musician and a mathematician may both investigate love, but through different instruments. No single person's way of knowing is enough.

9. The Gifts of God: Bearing One Another's Burdens

But toward what end are all these different gifts, dispositions, desires, and experiences being developed? What if their diversity is part of God's provision for His children to bear one another's burdens?

Perhaps the reason I cannot do what Axl Rose does, and Axl Rose cannot do what I do, is not merely that we are different. Perhaps our differences equip us to give one another things we could not otherwise obtain.

A person discovering a medical treatment may bear burdens for people she will never meet. A musician may console a stranger without knowing that stranger exists. An inventor, farmer, storyteller, mechanic, mother, or Sacred Clown may lighten another's burden or give someone strength.

The giver may not recognize the gift. The receiver may not recognize the giver. And neither may recognize God in the transaction. Wittingly or unwittingly.

What if the whole human family is already participating, imperfectly and often unwittingly, in an enormous system of bearing one another's burdens?

Mosiah 18:8–9 speaks of bearing one another's burdens, mourning with those who mourn, and comforting those in need. First Corinthians 12 describes many members and gifts in one mutually dependent body. Moroni 10 speaks of diverse gifts of God. Put them beside one another: gifts, mutual dependence, burden-bearing.

These scriptures do not explicitly establish every part of my speculation. But they illuminate one another. What if Zion is not merely the invention of an entirely new system, but also the recognition, redemption, coordination, and enlargement of the burden-bearing already occurring across the human family?

Not every contribution is good. Not every talent is used well. Not every enjoyable activity is divine service. Sometimes our gifts increase another person's burdens. That, too, belongs in the investigation.

Perhaps Jesus has been drawing people toward their particular gifts not simply so they become extraordinary individuals, but so they become gifts to one another. The destination is not merely MORE gifted people. It is MORE love made useful in the lives of others.

10. Everybody Already Has a Spiritual Groundhog Day

The menu taught me to notice recurrence. Now look at human attention. People wake, desire, remember, practice, repeat, and become increasingly shaped by what they attend to and love. This happens whether or not they recognize the process as spiritual.

Everybody already has some approximation of a recurring spiritual life: repeated attention, affection, habits, and formation. Not necessarily Christian. Not necessarily conscious. But real. Can these existing capacities be redirected toward remembering and loving God? That is Jesus Groundhog Day. That is the MORE Jesus = More Reality Experiment.

11. The First Commandment: There You Are!

Then I come upon the first and great commandment: love God with all your heart, soul, and mind (Matthew 22:37–38). Of course! The prime directive. Get MORE love of God than I had before. All you need is love, as the Beatles sang.

But here comes the One-Week Menu lesson again. Naming the fundamental principle is the easy part. Living its tradeoffs is hard. God is FRICKING INVISIBLE. He can be hard to hear. He barely seems to save sometimes. He appears to do head fakes. My experience of His presence is not always clear, predictable, or comfortable.

I can ask why I struggle to love Him more without pretending the difficulty has vanished. The first commandment is simple to state and inexhaustible to inhabit. Perhaps this is exactly the kind of problem I have been trained, by a thousand apparently ordinary investigations, to keep exploring.

12. The Sacred Clown Was Being Trained, Too

Wait a minute. I have been calling myself an investigator, a first-principles fellow, a pattern recognizer. All true enough. But I have left out something essential about my way of knowing: mashUP. Sacred Clown moves.

I do not merely discover patterns within established categories. I press apparently unrelated things together and see what happens. I take Adrian Monk's peculiar habits and explore vicarious service. I take Groundhog Day and turn it into a laboratory for recurring encounters with Jesus. I take a family menu and ask what it can teach me about the First Commandment. Rock songs, nano-marathons, pillows, television, gum wrappers, and the ordinary stuff of life become possible instruments of remembrance.

This is JESUS (MUDD)LER territory: deliberately pressing, twisting, combining, and reimagining ordinary ingredients in hopes of releasing extraordinary, Christ-reminding possibilities. Jesus does not always give me new ingredients. Perhaps He teaches me to taste the ones I have already been given.

The Sacred Clown makes the improbable move: What if these two things, which everyone knows do not belong together, are somehow speaking about the same underlying reality? The investigator then asks whether the collision reveals an actual principle or merely an amusing resemblance. The systems thinker asks whether it generalizes. And the Jesus-Greg experiment asks whether any of it helps me remember and love Jesus more.

The clown makes the strange move. The investigator searches for the underlying principle. The JESUS (MUDD)LER turns the discovery into something I can practice.

Maybe the willingness to look ridiculous is not incidental to this gift. Maybe it is part of what makes the gift possible. A person determined always to look sensible will avoid some of the very experiments that make my Jesus-Greg world possible.

And this widens the gifts question again. Some people uncover truths within categories. Some cross the boundaries between them. Some translate discoveries into experiences others can inhabit. No one way of knowing is sufficient. Perhaps God is working upon different people through all of these ways, wittingly or unwittingly.

So the investigator was not the only one under construction. The Sacred Clown was being trained, too.

13. The Gift May Be the Investigation

Suppose fifteen years of menu investigation produced only three useful principles. Would those years be wasted? No. I was also learning to recognize hidden recurrence, tolerate prolonged curiosity, weigh competing goods, and translate an implicit pattern into a teachable system.

Those capacities came with me when I turned toward Jesus. The menu may have improved family life. Norm's investigations may have clarified gifts and weaknesses. But perhaps both also prepared a particular kind of investigator for a particular kind of Jesus work.

And this possibility calls for humility. Other people are drawn toward realities I overlook. Other gifts may disclose dimensions of God's love that my first-principles approach cannot reach by itself. The body needs all its parts, not merely to be different, but to care for one another and bear burdens together.

14. The Question That Will Keep Me Busy

What if God is doing something analogous with everyone—working through distinctive gifts, dispositions, desires, and experiences, often without their recognizing Him, preparing each to contribute something the rest of the body needs? What if the point is the vast, often unwitting work of bearing one another's burdens?

What if I begin not merely by asking how to love God more, but by noticing how I have already been taught to love, attend, investigate, and desire—and asking how those capacities can be offered back to Him and outward to people whose burdens I might help carry?

This morning I see the One-Week Menu with Jesus mud on my eyes. I see Norm. I see 2015. I see the First Commandment. I see the wheels spinning round and round. Another story must begin. And perhaps, in an important sense, it already began a long time ago. The investigator may have been prepared not only to discover first principles but to help feed, comfort, and sustain others. Wittingly or unwittingly, the point is to bear one another's burdens.

POSTLUDE — FOR SAEBRA

Dear Saebra: The Man You Married, and the Cost of His Experiments

I want to explain something about Greg that may help make sense of the unusual things he has been doing over the years. And I want to begin by acknowledging something important.

Being married to Greg has sometimes required you to live with consequences you did not choose.

You are a more reserved person. You value privacy. You live in a community where people know you, your family, and your siblings. And then there is Greg: going live on Facebook, talking about Jesus in unusual ways, creating religious art that mixes sacred things with rock music, television, comedy, household objects, and sometimes downright ridiculous performances.

Other people may not understand what they are seeing. And you may be left wondering what they think, what they are saying, or how to explain any of it. That is not a small burden. Understanding Greg's motivations does not make your discomfort unreasonable.

Something interesting has emerged about Greg

For decades Greg has had an unusual appetite for investigating ordinary things. Most people simply decide what to eat for dinner. Greg spent well over a decade exploring the underlying structure of how families eat. He called his discovery the One-Week Menu. He noticed that most people already eat from a recurring collection of meals, whether they recognize it or not.

He investigated how to make that system tastier, healthier, less expensive, easier to prepare, and better coordinated. He did something similar with Dr. Norm Wood, exploring personality, gifts, weaknesses, and patterns of human behavior. When Greg becomes interested in something, he wants to investigate it, experiment with it, discover its hidden possibilities, and see how far he can take it.

Then he turned that energy toward Jesus

Around 2015, Greg began asking with increasing intensity: How can I know Jesus better, love Him more, and bring Him more fully into ordinary life? He approached that question much as he approached the One-Week Menu. He experimented.

What if an ordinary song, a walk, a pillow, a television program, or a funny performance could become an occasion for worship? What if ordinary life could become a set of reminders of Christ? He calls this the MORE Jesus = More Reality Experiment.

His mashUP and Sacred Clown methods deliberately bring unexpected things together. Some experiments are wonderful. Some are awkward. Some may be too strange for others to appreciate. But there is a recognizable pattern behind them. He is not simply trying to be different. He is trying to discover new ways of remembering and loving Jesus.

What Greg thinks he saw this morning

Greg began to see the menu years, the investigations with Norm, and his later Jesus work as connected. Perhaps God has been working upon him through his distinctive curiosity and temperament for a very long time. Perhaps the years of investigating ordinary things helped prepare the investigator for this later work.

That is Greg's spiritual interpretation of his experience, not something you are required to believe. But it matters deeply to him. He wonders whether God works through other people's personalities and gifts in equally distinctive ways.

Your way of being matters just as much

Saebra, your reserved nature is not a lesser way of living your faith. You do not need to become a Sacred Clown. You do not need to go live on Facebook, explain every experiment to your family, or agree that every experiment is inspired by Jesus.

If Greg's theory is right that God works through people's distinctive personalities and gifts, then your personality deserves the same respect he hopes people will extend to his. Your preference for privacy may be wisdom. Your caution may reveal consequences he overlooks. Your quietness may make room for kinds of love and worship his public experimentation cannot accomplish.

Something Greg must remember

Greg is investigating the First Commandment: loving God with all his heart, soul, and mind. But Jesus also taught the second great commandment: loving our neighbors as ourselves. Saebra is not merely Greg's neighbor. She is his wife.

If these experiments are intended to increase his love of Jesus, they should also help him become more attentive to the woman sharing his life. That means asking how public activities affect you, respecting your privacy, and distinguishing his freedom to experiment from your right not to become an unwilling participant. It means being willing to modify an experiment when its cost to you becomes too great.

Bearing one another's burdens includes the burdens nearest to home. The sincerity of Greg's spiritual intentions does not automatically justify every public expression of them.

That is not a rejection of his gifts. It is an invitation to use them more lovingly.

Perhaps this is another part of the experiment

The One-Week Menu taught Greg that a good system cannot optimize only one thing. It must accommodate taste, nutrition, expense, convenience, and the preferences of different people in the household. Marriage presents a similarly serious question: How can Greg honor his unusual gifts while honoring yours?

How can he experiment publicly without unnecessarily exposing you to embarrassment or unwanted attention? How can he remain himself while becoming more sensitive to the person who knows him most intimately? These are not interruptions to his investigation of Jesus. They may be among its most important experiments.

If Greg hopes his art will relieve burdens for strangers, he must attend to the burdens his public experiments may place on Saebra. Both the distant beneficiary and the person beside him count. Perhaps the greatest discovery will not be that Greg has found a religious justification for being unusual. Perhaps it will be that he can bring his unusual gifts to Jesus and become a more considerate husband in the process.

Because, Saebra, if the MORE Jesus Experiment is working, there ought to be MORE love coming your way, too. And you should not have to wait forever to experience it.

Second Postlude: Run and Not Be Weary

The Gifts of God and the Point of Ease

The MORE Jesus = More Reality Experiment

I keep returning to something Jesus has been teaching me. Rest.

I foray into one investigation after another, and then I return to this foundation: How does this give me greater rest in His kingdom?

“Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.” (Matthew 11:28)

And elsewhere comes the extraordinary promise that we shall “run, and not be weary” and “walk, and not faint.” (Isaiah 40:31; Doctrine and Covenants 89:20)

What if I have been understanding the reach of that promise too narrowly?

What if, in ways I have scarcely appreciated, the world is already full of people running and not being weary?

Not universal ease. Not ease in everything. But particular kinds of ease, given to particular people.

The Point of Ease

Throughout my life I have noticed people doing things that seem almost effortless to them. They get into the flow. They have stamina where I have resistance. They can keep going where I get tired. They understand things that I struggle to understand. They love doing things that I can barely make myself do.

We often call these differences personality, temperament, talent, aptitude, experience, or interest. But I am investigating what happens when I enter the religious realm and call them something else: the gifts of God.

The Doctrine and Covenants teaches that not all have every gift, that gifts are given differently, and that they are given so that all may be profited thereby. (Doctrine and Covenants 46:11–12)

God hasn't given me every gift. He hasn't given me every kind of stamina. I am an ENTP, not an INFJ. I can stretch into unfamiliar ways of being. I can practice. I can grow. Jesus may sometimes invite me to do exactly that.

But what if He doesn't always require me to become proficient at everything? What if He sometimes invites me to rest where He has already made me strong?

The Third-Grader Artist

Consider my art. I can paint like a third grader. And you know what? Painting like a third grader is easy! I can enjoy that. I can rest there.

I can also work to become a much more technically accomplished artist. Perhaps Jesus will invite me to do that someday. But He has not necessarily given me the time, desire, or particular gifts required to become one.

“And I will certainly do it when Jesus tells me to.”

And now AI arrives. Suddenly, with the help of these remarkable technologies, I can produce approximations of things that previously required skills I did not possess. Not necessarily the equivalent of a master artist's work. But approximations that can serve my purposes—sometimes wonderfully.

And this is only the beginning. What if emerging technologies allow more and more of God's children to accomplish things without enduring the weariness that formerly accompanied them? Not just in art. In writing. In building. In learning. In organizing. In communicating. In a thousand different endeavors.

What if technology becomes one of the ways that more people can run and not be weary?

The Rare Bird

Now consider my years with Norm Wood. Norm was teaching complex principles and practices involving measurement, mapping, accountability, and human behavior. I had resistance to some of his methods. And yet I kept coming back. Year after year. Why?

Partly because I could recognize something valuable in what he was teaching. And partly because I seem to have been given an unusual appetite for identifying fundamental principles.

I want to know: What is the powerful one here? What are the fundamentals? Can we get them down to five fingers? Three fingers? One? What is the E = mc² of this whole thing?

I did it with the One-Week Menu. I did it with home organization. I did it with Norm Wood. And now I am doing it with Jesus.

That doesn't mean I am more intelligent than people who don't enjoy these investigations. It may simply mean that God has given me a particular kind of desire, understanding, and stamina.

In those areas, I can sometimes run and not be weary. Not because the work requires no effort. But because something in me is wonderfully suited to the effort.

let me repeat: “In those areas, I can sometimes run and not be weary. Not because the work requires no effort. But because something in me is wonderfully suited to the effort.”

A REFLECTION ON DIFFERENT GIFTS

Your Daily Bread Is Not Necessarily Mine

This changes how I understand other people. Suppose I introduce someone to one of my favorite discoveries. I can see its value. I can hardly stop thinking about it. I want to explore every implication. And the other person? Not so much.

For years, I might have wondered why they couldn't see what I could see. Why wouldn't they investigate further? Why wouldn't they make the effort? But perhaps I have been asking the wrong question. Why should they?

There may be excellent reasons for them to stretch. Jesus may invite them to stretch. They may eventually discover something wonderful. But perhaps the thing I am offering is simply not their daily bread. It is mine.

And perhaps they have their own daily bread—things that nourish them, delight them, and draw forth their stamina, while leaving me relatively uninterested.

They may run and not be weary in places where I would become exhausted. And I may do the same in places where they would become exhausted. What a beautiful possibility!

Wrought Upon

Consider Christopher Columbus. Consider Axl Rose. Consider Norm Wood. Consider the quiet person who can patiently do something I find almost unbearable.

I am not proposing that every desire is righteous, that every gift is used well, or that everything a gifted person accomplishes is God's will. That requires discernment.

But I am proposing that we may overlook the extent to which people have been wrought upon—given distinctive combinations of desire, temperament, capacity, opportunity, and understanding.

We notice what they accomplish. But do we notice the gift that makes the accomplishment possible?

When I watch Axl Rose doing something that would be extraordinarily difficult for me, I can see more than talent. I can consider the possibility that I am witnessing one expression of the gifts of God.

A man running, in a particular realm, without the weariness I would experience attempting the same thing. And that makes me wonder how many such gifts surround me every day.

The Multitude of Gifts

Perhaps the promise that we shall run and not be weary is much wider than physical running. Physical stamina is a wonderful gift. But why suppose the principle ends there?

There is intellectual stamina. Creative stamina. Social stamina. Emotional stamina.

The stamina to care for children, solve mechanical problems, comfort the grieving, organize a household, investigate a difficult question, or patiently listen to someone whose mind works very differently from one's own.

There are gifts I possess. There are gifts I lack. There are gifts I can cultivate. And there are gifts I may never need to cultivate because someone else has already been wonderfully prepared to offer them.

Perhaps that is one way we bear one another's burdens. Perhaps that is one way Zion works.

The gift that allows you to run without weariness may relieve the burden of someone who would become weary doing the same thing. And the reverse may be equally true.

AN INVITATION TO REST

Returning to Rest

But here is where Jesus keeps bringing me back. I can turn even this discovery into another exhausting project.

I can try to persuade everyone that they should understand the gifts of God the way I do. I can insist that others appreciate the powerful principles I have found. I can demand that they run where I run. And suddenly I am weary again.

So I return to the foundation. Rest.

Perhaps Jesus is inviting me to stop resisting some of the very things He has made easy for me. Not every impulse is an invitation from Jesus. Sometimes He tells me to resist. Sometimes He asks me to do difficult things. Sometimes He asks me to wait. I must discern.

But when something good comes naturally—when it brings me joy, invites charity, and seems to make fruitful use of gifts I have been given—perhaps I should consider that ease itself may be part of the invitation.

Perhaps I need not apologize for being a rare bird. And perhaps I need not require other birds to fly like me.

The Saint at Rest

I have been experimenting with a definition: A saint is someone who enjoys the goodness of God.

And perhaps another: A saint is someone learning to rest with God, in God's ways.

Not someone who never works. Not someone who never struggles. Not someone who has mastered every gift.

But someone who increasingly recognizes the goodness God has already placed in the world, including the gifts distributed among His children. Someone who can receive those gifts with gratitude. Someone who can stop demanding that every person be given the same gifts. Someone who can stretch when Jesus invites stretching, and rest when Jesus invites rest.

I have spent decades investigating powerful principles. Perhaps one of the most powerful is also one of the simplest: Jesus has not asked me to become everybody.

And He has not asked everybody to become me. He has given us gifts. He has given us one another. And He invites us to come unto Him.

I have another name for Jesus. Rest.

And I am beginning to wonder whether discovering the gifts of God—my gifts, your gifts, and the gifts of people I have never met—is one of the ways He is teaching me to receive Him.

To run and not be weary. To walk and not faint. And to rest, increasingly, in His kingdom.

Third Postlude: A Mile Deep and an Inch Wide

The Weariness of Moving On

The MORE Jesus = More Reality Experiment

There is a chronic irritation I have experienced throughout my life.

Not an acute problem. Not some great tragedy. Just a persistent sense that the world keeps moving on before I am ready.

Move on. Move on. Move on.

We're studying fractions. Now we're studying proportions. Now the Pythagorean theorem. Now something else.

And I'm still back there with fractions.

Wait a minute.

Do I really understand what a fraction is?

Not merely how to manipulate one. Not merely how to get the correct answer on a test.

I mean, do I understand it?

Wait a minute. Do I really understand what a fraction is? Not merely how to manipulate one. Not merely how to get the correct answer on a test. I mean, do I understand it?

Thirteen Books and One Idea

I remember the experience of graduate school, where the expectation could be to read thirteen books, write reports, and keep moving through the curriculum.

Thirteen books!

But suppose there is one idea in the first book that could change my life.

Suppose I want to spend the next six months with it.

Suppose I want to take it apart, put it back together, test it against everything I know, and see whether it might be one of those powerful principles that reorganizes my understanding of reality.

Well, that's not how the course works.

We have twelve more books.

Move on.

And I understand why.

Schools have schedules. Teachers have responsibilities. Students have different needs. A curriculum cannot ordinarily wait for every student to finish every investigation.

But understanding the institutional necessity doesn't eliminate the personal cost.

Sometimes I have spent much of my life feeling hurried past the very things I most wanted to understand.

A Mile Wide and an Inch Deep?

People sometimes criticize an education or an investigation for being a mile wide and an inch deep.

But I am beginning to understand that I have two very different modes.

In one mode, I am perfectly happy to be a third-grade artist. I can make something, enjoy it, and move on.

There is rest in that.

But when I encounter something that seems fundamental—something with the potential to explain a thousand other things—I want to reverse the proportions.

An inch wide and a mile deep.

I want to find the E = mc².

The powerful one.

The small and simple thing by which great things might be brought to pass.

And once I find it, I don't necessarily want another idea.

I want to inhabit this one.

I want to fashion it to my soul.

Professor Young and the Fractions

I remember telling my graduate statistics professor, Larry Young, about an experience that was both wonderful and irritating.

I had begun to understand fractions in a way I had not understood them before.

An intuitive understanding.

An aha!

But why was this happening in graduate school?

Why hadn't I been allowed—or helped—to dwell with this fundamental idea long enough to understand it years earlier?

Perhaps I had learned enough to pass the tests.

Perhaps I had learned enough to advance.

But advancing and understanding are not necessarily the same thing.

And I wonder how many other fundamentals I have hurried past.

How much of my education was spent moving forward before the foundation had become part of me?

Trying On Too Many Clothes

Now I am anticipating another year of New Testament study with my fellow Latter-day Saints.

And I love the New Testament.

But already I can feel the familiar tension.

There is so much to cover.

So many chapters. So many stories. So many doctrines. So many wonderful things.

And my heart says:

Could we just choose one?

Could we find one teaching of Jesus and spend the rest of the year with it?

Try it on.

Walk around in it.

Sleep in it.

Wake up with it.

Discover how it fits.

Alter it—not the teaching itself, but the way I understand and inhabit it—until it becomes part of my spiritual wardrobe.

Why must we keep trying on so many clothes?

What if we found one that fit beautifully and spent our time learning to wear it well?

I realize that other people may need something different.

Some are nourished by breadth. Some discover their gifts through variety. Some need the survey before they can recognize what deserves deeper attention.

But I am learning to recognize my own daily bread.

And sometimes my daily bread is one verse.

For a very long time.

The Thick of Thin Things

We can become so busy with important things that we never spend much time with any one of them.

We live in the thick of thin things.

Even our efforts to help one another can take on this character.

We identify a need. We offer a solution. We move on.

Another need. Another solution. Another program.

But what if the person needs us to stay?

What if the problem requires more listening than doing?

What if understanding one another takes months or years?

There is a kind of love that cannot be hurried.

There is a kind of knowledge that cannot be obtained by passing through.

And there is a kind of common consent that requires us to remain in conversation long after we would ordinarily prefer to vote and move on.

I understand why majority rule is attractive. Reaching deeper agreement can be exhausting, and sometimes decisions genuinely cannot wait.

But what might we discover if we had more patience for understanding?

What if we spent less time trying to win decisions and more time discovering the principles that could bring us together?

Perhaps some of our disagreements persist because we have never stayed with one another long enough to understand what is fundamental.

WHEN GIFTS AND SYSTEMS MOVE AT DIFFERENT SPEEDS

The Gift and the Irritation

Here is the connection to my earlier postlude.

I have been investigating the possibility that God has given different people different kinds of stamina.

And I seem to have been given unusual stamina for certain investigations.

Norm Wood saw something of that.

The One-Week Menu revealed something of that.

My home-organization investigations revealed something of that.

And now my investigations with Jesus are revealing it again.

But the very gift that makes one kind of work restful can make another kind of environment exhausting.

I can spend years investigating one fundamental principle and feel wonderfully alive.

Yet I can become weary sitting through a rapid succession of interesting lessons.

Not because the lessons are bad.

Not because the teachers are doing anything wrong.

Not because the other students are less serious than I am.

But because the pace of the institution and the pace of my gift are not always the same.

Perhaps I have spent too much of my life interpreting that mismatch as a defect in myself—or a defect in everybody else.

Maybe neither conclusion is necessary.

Jesus and the One Thing

I keep returning to the Savior's invitation to Mary and Martha.

“One thing is needful.” (Luke 10:42)

I don't suppose that verse settles every question about education, curriculum, or the proper pace of learning.

But I find myself drawn to it.

One thing.

What if I could spend a year investigating one thing?

What if I could spend a lifetime?

What if my investigation of Jesus is not a failure to move on but an invitation to remain?

To abide?

To let something become so deeply familiar that it changes how I see everything else?

I have been walking what I call the Inference Bridge.

And perhaps the bridge is not meant to carry me hurriedly from one proposition to the next.

Perhaps sometimes Jesus invites me to stop in the middle.

Look around.

Notice what is already there.

And rest.

Permission to Stay

I don't want to make my preferred way of learning into a universal commandment.

That would contradict what I have been learning about the gifts of God.

Some people are gifted at surveying vast territories.

Some are gifted at mastering specialized details.

Some can move quickly among many responsibilities without losing their sense of purpose.

And some of us want to stop beside one small, beautiful thing and investigate it until it opens into a universe.

There may be room in God's kingdom for all of us.

So perhaps I can stop resenting every occasion when the world moves faster than I want.

And perhaps I can also stop assuming that I must always move at the world's pace.

I can attend the class.

I can enjoy the breadth.

I can receive what others have to offer.

And then I can return to my one thing.

My daily bread.

My powerful one.

My E = mc².

Because perhaps this, too, is part of learning to run and not be weary:

Knowing when Jesus is inviting me to move on—and knowing when He is inviting me to stay.

And sometimes, I suspect, He is saying:

You don't have to hurry, Greg.

We can stay here awhile.
