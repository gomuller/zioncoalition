Dear Brother Andy: Back to Graduate School with Jesus

A personal introduction and a friendly Latter-day Saint scholarly counterargument

Brother Andy, whom I love and admire—

Finally, finally, Jesus is having me engage more directly with your beliefs—your best guesses, your sacred imaginings.

But not in the way I would have supposed He would.

Jesus surprises me at every turn.

A traditional scholar might read your latest essay thoroughly, ponder it independently (with Jesus), examine the scriptures and historical sources, and then carefully construct a thoughtful response.

But Jesus is having me do something a little different.

He's taking me back to graduate school.

I remember graduate school well. I was often overwhelmed by how far I had to stretch my mind. I wasn't the sharpest tool in the shed. I was surrounded by remarkably bright graduate students, many of whom seemed far better prepared than I was.

Sometimes I felt like the last kid picked for the graduate-school team. And, in a sense, I probably was. I've always been pretty good at working the system, finding my way through doors I might not otherwise have qualified to enter. We ENTPs are a wily bunch.

Now, I wasn't without intellectual abilities. But I was often playing catch-up.

Professors would assign ten books in a semester—dense, complicated, intellectually demanding books. And I was poor, raising four babies during those graduate-school years, and coming into the program without having read War and Peace and many of the other classics that my fellow students seemed to have absorbed long before arriving.

Sometimes it was all I could do just to understand what an author was saying.

Forget critically evaluating the argument! Forget identifying its weaknesses! And forget constructing some brilliant counterargument!

I was lucky if I could get my mind around the author's basic ideas well enough to write a short paper and survive the seminar.

And so, I cheated.

Well, sort of.

When I couldn't possibly read everything assigned, I'd head to the library and find scholarly book reviews. I'd read several reviews of the assigned book, looking for two things:

  1. What, basically, was this author arguing?
  2. What were the strongest counterarguments other scholars were making?

Armed with that, I'd walk into a three-hour graduate seminar and participate as though I'd done all the reading.

Was I a fraud?

Yeah. Kinda.

It's somewhat comforting to recognize that we're probably all a little fraudulent in different ways at different times in our lives.

But I survived graduate school—sometimes by the skin of my teeth. And along the way, I actually learned a great deal.

Which brings me back to you, Brother Andy.

Your writings are familiar territory for me. Not because I already understand everything you're proposing, but because they remind me of those intellectually demanding books I encountered in graduate school.

You're presenting substantial, interconnected ideas that require more intellectual work than I can easily give them. And I'm still not the sharpest tool in the shed.

So Jesus is having me cheat again!

Only this time, instead of taking Andy's book to the university library, we're taking Andy's Facebook posts to the ChatGPT section.

And here's the assignment I sense Jesus giving me:

"Hey, Greg, let's feed Andy's writings into AI and ask what a thoughtful, fair-minded, faithful Latter-day Saint scholar might say in response."

It's a TWO-FER!

First, I get help understanding what Andy is actually proposing—his evolving beliefs, his best guesses about Zion, Joseph Smith, Brigham Young, priesthood government, and the kingdom of Jesus Christ.

Second, I get to hear the strongest reasonable counterarguments to those proposals.

Not cheap shots. Not dismissals. Not institutional defensiveness.

Good, thoughtful counterarguments from someone who takes Andy seriously.

And so that's what I did.

But before sharing the results, I want to say something important.

Andy, I love that you boldly share your beliefs—your best guesses, your sacred imaginings. I love that you're willing to put them out in the open, invite discussion, and allow others to examine them.

And I especially love that you extend that same freedom to me.

You've often used the phrase "not use a finger of control."

And I believe you mean it.

You aren't trying to take my best guesses away from me. You're willing to let me keep exploring, experimenting, and arriving—on whatever timetable Jesus has for me—at whatever understanding He wants me to have.

That means a great deal to me.

Because asking another person to believe something substantially different from what they have come to believe is an enormous request.

Especially when those beliefs have been growing in their heart for years.

I think Jesus understands that better than anyone.

I suspect He gives us plenty of time to discover new truths. A lifetime, perhaps. Maybe two or three lifetimes! Whatever His timetable might be.

And I love Alma 32 as a model for that process.

Plant the seed. Nourish it. Give it room. Observe what happens. Does it swell? Does it enlarge the soul? Does it enlighten understanding? Does it become delicious?

And then keep nourishing it.

Some of the seeds Jesus has planted in my heart have been growing for many years now. I have come to treasure them, not simply because I believe them, but because of what I have experienced as I've tried living by them.

And here's something I want you to know without the slightest ambiguity:

Andy, I am completely, totally, 100 percent open to believing exactly what you believe.

As soon as Jesus tells me to.

No institutional loyalty, no intellectual pride, no attachment to my current understanding should stand in the way of that.

But until He does, I'm going to keep nurturing the seeds already growing in my heart.

And I'm also going to keep watching yours.

For years and years to come, I hope.

Not merely watching what happens around you, but looking for what happens because of the beliefs you're planting and cultivating.

Causation, not merely correlation.

What fruits do these beliefs produce in your life? In your relationships? In your capacity to love God and your neighbor? In the people and communities influenced by your work?

And are those fruits distinguishable from—and perhaps greater than—the fruits growing in the lives of people who hold substantially different beliefs?

That's a difficult comparison, of course. None of us can perfectly isolate the effects of a belief from all the other things Jesus may be doing in our lives.

But it seems to me a worthy, long-term Alma 32 experiment.

If I see extraordinary fruits that appear to grow specifically from your best guesses, I'll have good reason to examine those seeds even more closely.

If I don't, I'll be less inclined to uproot the seeds Jesus has already been growing in my heart in favor of the ones you're cultivating.

And I hope you'll afford me the same freedom.

Meanwhile, I'm delighted that we can keep comparing notes, asking questions, making our sacred guesses, and watching what Jesus does with all of it.

So, Brother Andy, here we go.

Back to graduate school.

Back to the library.

Only this time, Jesus has provided a rather remarkable research assistant.

And below is what happened when I asked ChatGPT:

"What is the strongest friendly LDS scholar's counterargument to Andy's essay, 'The Church Joseph Was Building Is Not the Church Brigham Built—and That's OK'?"

I offer it not as my own completed analysis, nor as a verdict against your ideas, but as a way for me to begin engaging them more seriously.

And perhaps, along the way, to understand both your beliefs and my own a little better.

With love, admiration, and a graduate student's continuing appreciation for a good shortcut—

Greg

Still surviving graduate school with Jesus, by the skin of my teeth.

Part Two · The friendly scholar's response

What Is the Friendly LDS Scholar's Counter to This?

A thoughtful, faithful Latter-day Saint scholar could offer a substantial counterargument to this essay without dismissing its concerns, denying the historical changes, or questioning the authors' devotion to Jesus Christ.

I think the strongest response would be:

“You have correctly identified a tension between Joseph Smith's vision of Zion and the institutional Church that developed under Brigham Young. But you have not demonstrated that institutional development was a departure from Zion rather than part of the divinely authorized process of building it.”

That distinction is fundamental.

The friendly LDS scholar's counterargument

1. Joseph Smith was building a Church with hierarchy, not merely a federation of autonomous communities.

Your essay emphasizes common consent, local governance, economic independence, and direct accountability to Jesus Christ.

A faithful scholar might respond:

Joseph Smith himself established the First Presidency, Quorum of the Twelve Apostles, high councils, bishops, and priesthood offices. He taught that these offices possessed genuine spiritual authority.

D&C 107 describes an elaborate governing structure. D&C 28 rejects the idea that anyone can receive commandments for the entire Church independently of the person appointed to lead it.

D&C 42, 51, and 82 also describe organized stewardship and the administration of consecrated property.

The counter: Brigham Young did not invent hierarchical priesthood government. Joseph established it.

The scholar would concede that Joseph envisioned extensive common consent and local responsibility, but argue that these operated within an authorized priesthood structure, not as alternatives to it.

2. The polygamy argument depends on a disputed reading of the historical record.

Your essay contrasts the 1835 statement condemning polygamy with D&C 132, published after Joseph's death.

The scholar would acknowledge that the 1835 statement was indeed published during Joseph's lifetime.

But they would add that substantial historical evidence indicates Joseph privately introduced plural marriage before his death in 1844.

The 1835 statement was a declaration on marriage, rather than a revelation equivalent in status to today's D&C 132. Its presence in the Doctrine and Covenants nevertheless makes the tension important.

The counter: Brigham publicly institutionalized a practice Joseph had already privately introduced. Therefore, the difference cannot simply be described as Joseph's monogamous Church versus Brigham's polygamous Church.

A friendly scholar might even agree that the secrecy, public denials, and later institutional developments deserve serious moral and historical examination.

But those difficulties do not establish that Brigham abandoned Joseph's revealed program.

3. The tithing argument is intriguing, but D&C 119 does not conclusively establish your proposed economic system.

Your essay argues that Joseph established an annual tithe on property interest rather than labor or income.

The scholar would point to the wording of D&C 119:

“Those who have thus been tithed shall pay one-tenth of all their interest annually; and this shall be a standing law unto them forever.”

But verse 1 also requires an initial contribution of surplus property.

The meaning of interest in nineteenth-century usage, and its practical application in early Church history, requires historical investigation. It cannot simply be equated with a modern property-value-based tax without additional evidence.

The scholar would also distinguish between a change in administrative implementation and a change in revealed doctrine.

The counter: Even if Joseph's earliest implementation differed substantially from modern income-based tithing, that does not prove subsequent prophets lacked authority to adapt its administration.

And the claim that any tithe on labor is an abomination goes beyond what D&C 119 explicitly says.

4. The wilderness analogy might actually support Brigham Young.

This could be one of the scholar's most interesting responses.

Your essay compares Brigham's Church to Israel receiving a preparatory law after failing to receive the higher law.

The scholar might say:

"Perhaps the Saints did lose opportunities to establish Zion in Missouri. D&C 105 explicitly says they had not learned obedience and unity sufficiently to redeem Zion. But the Lord did not therefore abandon His covenant people or revoke His authority."

D&C 136 is particularly important because it presents instructions given through Brigham Young as the word and will of the Lord.

The scholar could argue that the migration west, establishment of settlements, construction of temples, and preservation of a covenant community were not simply a retreat into a lower law.

They were genuine steps toward Zion under extremely difficult conditions.

The counter: A wilderness period is not necessarily a counterfeit kingdom. It may be an indispensable stage in God's continuing work.

5. Common consent does not mean unanimous consent or the absence of presiding authority.

Your essay envisions autonomous communities whose representatives are chosen through patient discussion and unanimous consent.

The scholar would applaud the emphasis on deliberation, love, and voluntary participation.

But D&C 26:2 says Church business is to be conducted by common consent; it does not require unanimity in every decision.

Likewise, D&C 107 describes decisions made by quorums, including provisions for unanimity in certain circumstances, while retaining presiding offices and established procedures.

The scholar might also note that D&C 1:19–22, cited in the essay, addresses the Lord's work through the weak and simple, not a constitutional design for autonomous local communities.

The counter: Your proposed system may be an attractive political and economic ideal, but its specific architecture is not plainly established by the revelations you cite.

6. D&C 121 condemns unrighteous dominion, not hierarchy itself.

This is probably the most powerful scriptural challenge to the essay's statement:

“No hierarchy will ever build Zion.”

D&C 121 condemns compulsion, pride, and control exercised in unrighteousness.

But verses 41–46 also describe how priesthood influence ought to operate:

“By persuasion, by long-suffering, by gentleness and meekness, and by love unfeigned.”

A faithful scholar would argue that Joseph's revelation does not abolish leadership. It transforms the moral conditions under which leadership may be exercised.

A bishop who serves through persuasion and love is not necessarily an obstacle to Zion.

An apostle who teaches, counsels, and ministers without coercion is not necessarily exercising unrighteous dominion.

The counter: The problem is not hierarchy as such. The problem is dominion that replaces love with coercion.

And that distinction matters because even an autonomous community can develop powerful informal hierarchies.

7. Temple ordinances and priesthood authority are not optional accessories to Zion.

Your essay says:

“Not even ‘authority’ and temple ordinances can establish Zion—it is God’s government and economy that lays the framework…”

The scholar might agree that ordinances alone cannot produce a Zion society.

But Joseph Smith's later Nauvoo teachings placed temple ordinances, sealing relationships, and priesthood keys at the center of his developing vision.

D&C 124 links temple worship with ordinances the Lord intended to restore.

The scholar would argue that the temple is not merely a ceremonial institution alongside Zion's government and economy.

It is part of how God creates a covenant people capable of becoming Zion.

The counter: You may be separating Zion's economic and governmental structures from its priesthood and temple foundations more sharply than Joseph himself did.

8. The Daniel interpretation is possible as a theological proposal, but historically unproven.

Your essay asks whether Daniel's seventy weeks anticipated Joseph and Hyrum's deaths in 1844 and the abandonment of Zion.

A scholar would distinguish three things: what Daniel meant in its ancient context, how later Christians interpreted Daniel, and how a modern Latter-day Saint might apply Daniel typologically.

Daniel 9 is ordinarily studied in connection with events involving Jerusalem, the sanctuary, covenant violation, and the crises surrounding the Jewish people in antiquity. Christian interpreters have also connected it to Jesus Christ.

There is no widely established textual or chronological argument demonstrating that the prophecy specifically identifies the martyrdom of Joseph and Hyrum.

Likewise, Daniel 2's stone cut without hands has long been interpreted by Latter-day Saints as the latter-day kingdom of God.

The counter: Your interpretation is imaginative and potentially meaningful as a typology, but it cannot yet bear the historical and doctrinal weight your essay places upon it.

9. The scholar's ultimate objection: You may be making your preferred form of government a prerequisite for Zion.

The essay proposes that autonomous families and communities, operating through consensus and local stewardship, constitute the government of King Jesus.

A scholar might respond:

"Zion is defined in scripture primarily by the spiritual condition of its people, not by a particular constitutional arrangement."

Moses 7:18 describes a people who were of one heart and one mind, dwelt in righteousness, and had no poor among them.

That description certainly has economic and social implications.

But does it require the elimination of all presiding offices? Does it prohibit centralized financial administration? Does it demand a particular property-tax structure?

The scholar would say the scriptures do not establish those conclusions.

The counter: You have presented a compelling model of Zion, but you have not yet demonstrated that it is the only divinely authorized model.

Where the friendly scholar might actually agree

There are significant areas of common ground.

The Church's own historical materials acknowledge changes in the administration of tithing and the development of Church government. The Joseph Smith Papers also document the 1835 marriage statement and Joseph's 1843 plural-marriage revelation.

Furthermore, an LDS instructional manual explicitly describes the law of consecration as having been withdrawn for a time and tithing as a subsequent law. That gives the essay's higher-law/lower-law analogy a genuine point of contact with traditional LDS teaching.

A scholar could therefore agree that the Saints have not yet fully achieved Zion, that institutional traditions can become obstacles to further revelation, and that unrighteous dominion can corrupt even divinely authorized offices.

The disagreement concerns whether those failures demonstrate that the institutional Church must eventually relinquish its presiding authority.

The three questions I would put back to the scholar

I think the most productive discussion would turn on three questions:

  1. If Joseph established priesthood hierarchy, did he intend it to be permanent government or temporary scaffolding? D&C 107 establishes presiding offices, but it also requires unanimity in decisions of certain presiding quorums and describes equality of authority among them.
  2. If tithing is a standing law forever, what exactly is permanent? The ten-percent obligation? The definition of interest? The administrative structure? Or the underlying covenant to consecrate our increase for God's purposes?
  3. If Zion is a society of one heart and one mind, how do we know when priesthood leadership is facilitating that condition—and when institutional authority is inadvertently preventing it?

These are questions a serious LDS scholar should be able to engage without dismissing the essay as simply anti-Church.

My assessment

The essay's strongest argument is not that Brigham Young changed Joseph Smith's Church. That is historically demonstrable in numerous institutional and administrative respects.

Its strongest argument is that the institutions which preserve a religious people may not be identical to the ultimate society those institutions are intended to produce.

Its weakest argument is the leap from that observation to the categorical assertion that no hierarchy can ever build Zion.

And its most important unresolved question is whether the Lord intends priesthood hierarchy to be progressively transformed, voluntarily relinquished, or perfected through Christlike service.

A friendly LDS scholar would probably conclude:

"I agree that Jesus Christ is King, that Zion remains unfinished, and that our institutions must never become ends in themselves. But I believe Joseph Smith established priesthood keys and ordinances as essential instruments of that unfinished work. Brigham Young preserved and developed those instruments under divine direction. Their imperfections do not make them merely a lower-law substitute for Zion. They may be among the very means by which the Lord intends to establish it."

That, I think, is the strongest faithful LDS counterargument—and it leaves open a genuinely interesting conversation about what the Church is ultimately supposed to become.
