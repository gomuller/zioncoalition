Prelude — The Front Stage and the Back Stage

In 2015, amid a period Greg describes as upheaval and religious awakening, a presentation shared by Brad VanDyke became part of his developing language of returning. In 2017, Greg sent his Captains a film called The Breath in All Things and explained that the front-stage language of breath was a deliberate approximation of his backstage language, The King of Kings in All Things. This edition revisits that experiment without pretending that Kabat-Zinn was teaching Christian doctrine.

The governing proposal is not that oxygen and Jesus are interchangeable entities. It is that the reliable, ordinary act of returning attention to bodily breathing can be repurposed as a rehearsal for remembering Christ. That analogy invites discoveries and also has boundaries: the source's contemplative methods, Greg's private interpretation, the claims of scripture, and verifiable historical facts remain distinct.

The notes are exploratory rather than authoritative scripture. They attempt to hear what happens when mindfulness meets the Book of Mormon's promise to always remember Christ (Moroni 4:3), the Gospel invitation to rest (Matthew 11:28–30), the seed experiment of Alma 32, and the invitation to see Christ in others (Matthew 25:40). Their most important test is not imaginative cleverness but the fruits of love and responsibility.

Postlude — Returning, and Returning Again

Read as a whole, this talk moves through four enlargements: from productivity to being, from being to bodily attention, from a narrow breath anchor to the whole field of life, and from solitary practice to institutions and relationships. Greg's 2017 film proposed a fifth enlargement: use the exercise as a code or rehearsal for Christward attention. The code becomes most compelling when it changes the way a person treats another person, rather than merely increasing the number of religious associations one can invent.

There is a crucial asymmetry. Breathing goes on without our remembering it; discipleship involves choice, discernment, and sometimes costly action. A Christian can thank God for breath without claiming every sensation is a communication from God. A Jesus TV image can open the imagination without proving that the scene was staged by Jesus. These limits do not weaken the project; they protect its honesty.

The Foundation Practice that survives is beautifully small: notice that attention has wandered; return without theatrical self-condemnation; become present to the person, task, or creature before you; repeat. It can be rehearsed before a meal, a meeting, a fence repair, a song, or a quiet rest. The lifelong task is to discover whether the repeated turning yields greater charity, steadier judgment, and more willingness to bear burdens.

In this sense the project is not simply a Christian commentary on a Buddhist-influenced meditation lesson. It is an experiment in what a human being chooses to place at the center of a world. The Captain cannot command the wind or guarantee revelation. He can tune the instrument, make room for the neighbor, test his inferences, gather pearls of gratitude, and return again.
