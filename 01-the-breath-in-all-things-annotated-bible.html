About the Voices in These Margins

We are living through an unsettled moment in the history of writing and art: AI can help people express ideas, but it also raises questions about authorship, authenticity, and whose voice we are hearing. The Sacrament and the Art of Returning — A Jesus-Greg Mashup

What might the weekly Latter-day Saint sacrament and Jon Kabat-Zinn’s mindfulness teaching illuminate in each other? The connection is not a claim that mindfulness and the ordinance are interchangeable, nor that Kabat-Zinn intended a Christian lesson. It is a comparison of their architecture of repeated returning. One practice trains attention in the present moment; the other invites disciples to remember Jesus Christ through a recurring covenantal ordinance. Their purposes differ, but their rhythms can speak to one another.

1. Forgetting, noticing, returning

In the Google talk, Kabat-Zinn describes a mind that wanders and is brought back, again and again, without self-condemnation. The point is not to become a person whose attention never drifts. It is to learn what to do upon noticing that it has. The sacrament, too, makes sense in a world where disciples forget, become distracted, sin, and need to turn again toward Christ. Its weekly recurrence does not announce that everyone has succeeded at perfect remembrance; it provides another occasion to recommit.

There is an important distinction: Christian repentance is more than an attentional reset, and it may require confession, restitution, and changed conduct. Still, Kabat-Zinn’s refusal to make wandering an occasion for contempt offers a useful analogy. The disciple need not turn the discovery of forgetfulness into another reason to despair. The discovery can become an invitation to return.

2. Tuning the instrument before the symphony

Kabat-Zinn compares meditation to musicians tuning before they play. Even excellent players with excellent instruments must listen, adjust, and come into relation with one another. For a Latter-day Saint, sacrament meeting can serve as a weekly tuning of attention and intention. Bread and water direct the worshipper toward Christ’s body and blood, the covenant, and the promise of His Spirit.

The music, however, is not confined to the chapel. It is played afterward: in the kitchen, the workplace, the car, the difficult conversation, and the unobserved act of kindness. The quiet moments while the emblems are passed can be a tuning interval, but the measure of that tuning is what happens when ordinary life resumes.

3. “Always remember Him”: from formal practice to everyday life

The sacramental prayers in Moroni 4–5 and Doctrine and Covenants 20 give the weekly act a horizon far beyond Sunday: a willingness to always remember Him. Kabat-Zinn similarly distinguishes formal meditation from the larger practice of bringing awareness into the life one is actually living. Neither framework is satisfied merely by completing its scheduled exercise.

Here the Jesus-Greg experiment finds a particularly fertile connection. A sacrament meeting is a formal, communal anchor. The rest of the week offers countless informal occasions to remember: a breath, a face, a meal, a task, an interruption. The ordinance does not make every breath a sacrament. Rather, the sacramental promise may change what a person chooses to do with the next breath, the next encounter, the next opportunity to love.

4. Interrupting the “story of me”

Kabat-Zinn notices how easily the mind becomes absorbed in a running narrative of personal success, failure, importance, or inadequacy. The sacrament proposes a different center of gravity. Worshippers take upon themselves the name of Christ; they do not gather to celebrate the supremacy of their individual résumés. The same emblems pass among people with different fortunes, titles, burdens, and histories.

This is not the erasure of personality or the denial of suffering. It is a recurring invitation to belong to a story larger than the self. In Greg’s language, it is a chance to loosen the grip of a Greg-centric WORLD and rehearse living in a Jesus-centric one. A meaningful test follows: does the ritual make its participant less self-absorbed and more ready to bear another person’s burden?

6. Jesus Groundhog Day: building a world that invites return

The breath offers a moment-by-moment opportunity to return to awareness. The weekly sacrament offers a recurring covenantal opportunity to return to Jesus Christ. Greg’s Jesus-Greg WORLD extends this architecture into the surroundings and repetitions of ordinary life. Rather than relying on an occasional flash of religious attention, he deliberately makes rooms, songs, meals, doors, pillows, art, and familiar routines into invitations to remember Jesus again.

Greg’s idea of Jesus Groundhog Day names the gift hidden in recurring scenes. We wake, eat, work, rest, encounter neighbors, and pass through the same places over and over. The repetition need not be spiritual stagnation. Each return can be another rehearsal: what might it look like to notice, thank, imitate, or worship Christ more in this scene than I did the last time? The project is not to achieve uninterrupted attention through force. It is to build a Jesus-centric WORLD in which forgetting is met by increasingly numerous and inviting opportunities to remember.

7. Jesus Nostalgia: returning to a life already lived

There is another kind of return, one that neither the present-moment breath nor the weekly calendar fully captures: returning through memory. Greg calls this Jesus Nostalgia. He revisits events from his own past—sometimes decades old—and tries to remember them more fully, with MORE Jesus than before. A familiar memory becomes a fresh occasion for gratitude, sacred imagination, and the possibility of recognizing Christ’s influence where Greg did not recognize it at the time.

The past event is not rewritten by the act of remembering. What can change is the attention brought to it now: what was overlooked, whom Greg might thank, what goodness was present, what burden was borne, what Christ-reminding meaning might be discovered. Such interpretations can be cherished as faith-filled readings without requiring that every remembered detail become publicly demonstrable evidence of divine intervention. His 500 Favorite Times on Earth project offers a natural home for this practice: a growing collection of lived scenes that can be revisited, reconsidered, and received anew.

Thus the architecture of returning works in more than one direction. Mindfulness returns to the present. The sacrament returns the worshipper to covenantal remembrance. Jesus Groundhog Day returns to recurring scenes. Jesus Nostalgia returns to remembered scenes. Together, they suggest a distinctive aspiration of My Jesus-Greg WORLD: not only to remember Jesus more often in the life Greg is living, but to remember MORE Jesus in the life he has already lived.
