What If the Greatest Treasure Is Belief-Based?

Yes. I think there is a serious thought experiment here that does not require us first to settle whether LDS temple claims are metaphysically true.

Suppose we grant this premise:

What if one of the greatest treasures available in mortality is a set of beliefs that changes the experienced meaning of mortality itself?

Then ask a deliberately secular observer—an anthropologist, sociologist, psychologist, philosopher, or simply a good-hearted atheist—to examine what happens when someone deeply believes the temple story and actually inhabits it.

That observer could reasonably say:

“Greg, I don't believe your temple cosmology. I don't think your ancestors are literally participating, I don't know that covenants have eternal force, and I don't think you've established that God authorized these rituals.

But damn—you may have stumbled onto one hell of a placebo.

Because those beliefs cause you to experience your body, marriage, ancestors, death, responsibility, suffering, strangers, time, and your own identity differently. If the beliefs consistently make you more loving, courageous, grateful, faithful, connected, and alive, I can't dismiss what they're doing simply because I reject your explanation for why they work.”

And importantly, “placebo” is almost too weak a word. Many of these benefits don't depend upon an imaginary physiological mechanism. They arise through attention, ritual, narrative, identity, community, memory, expectation, commitment, and repeated behavior. Those are real causal mechanisms whether God exists or not.

So let's temporarily bracket the truth question.

The Temple as a Treasure Chest of Beliefs

Here are 93 potential gifts contained in deeply inhabiting LDS temple belief.

Identity and existence

  1. I am a child of God. Your fundamental identity precedes occupation, income, popularity, success, illness, aging, and failure.

  2. I existed before birth. Mortality becomes a chapter rather than the beginning of the book.

  3. I will exist after death. Death becomes passage rather than annihilation.

  4. My existence is intentional. You aren't merely cosmic debris that temporarily became conscious.

  5. My body has sacred significance. Embodiment becomes part of a divine education rather than an unfortunate biological accident.

  6. Mortality has a curriculum. Difficulty can be interpreted as something through which one can learn rather than merely something that happened.

  7. I am becoming something. Life acquires developmental direction.

  8. Human beings possess extraordinary potential. The person beside you is potentially vastly more significant than present appearances suggest.

  9. My deepest identity isn't determined by the marketplace. Money and status lose some power to define personal worth.

  10. Aging doesn't mean I am becoming less real. The deteriorating body isn't the entire person.

Time

  1. My life belongs inside a much larger story. Seventy or eighty years no longer constitute the whole narrative.

  2. Today matters eternally. Ordinary choices acquire significance.

  3. The past isn't completely inaccessible. Through remembrance, genealogy, story, and ritual, the dead remain psychologically present.

  4. The future reaches backward into today. Eternal aspirations influence present conduct.

  5. Repeated ritual interrupts ordinary time. You periodically leave errands, bills, Facebook, politics, and work and enter sacred time.

  6. Life possesses beginnings, transitions, and destinations. Ritual gives structure to otherwise amorphous existence.

Ancestors

This may be one of your strongest examples.

  1. My ancestors matter. People who disappeared from ordinary social consciousness become worth remembering.

  2. I should learn their names. Genealogy turns anonymous history into persons.

  3. Their lives contributed to mine. Gratitude stretches backward through generations.

  4. I can symbolically do something for the dead. Instead of merely remembering ancestors, the believer acts on their behalf.

  5. The dead aren't simply gone. The relationship between generations becomes psychologically porous.

  6. My family existed before I arrived. Radical individualism gets challenged.

  7. I am somebody's ancestor. Suddenly today's conduct becomes tomorrow's inheritance.

  8. Future descendants may remember me. That can change how one lives now.

  9. Healing can be imagined intergenerationally. You can ask what patterns should be preserved and what patterns should end with you.

An atheist genealogist could reject proxy ordinances entirely and still say:

“A ritual that repeatedly makes living people remember forgotten dead people? That's culturally fascinating—and potentially beautiful.”

Marriage and family

  1. Marriage can be imagined beyond death. The relationship is placed against an enormous horizon.

  2. My spouse isn't disposable. Covenant language introduces resistance to consumer approaches to relationships.

  3. Family relationships deserve deliberate cultivation.

  4. Parenthood participates in something larger than reproduction.

  5. Sexuality receives meaning beyond immediate appetite.

  6. Fidelity becomes an identity rather than merely a rule.

  7. Family history becomes sacred history.

  8. Ordinary family life can become spiritually consequential.

  9. Reconciliation acquires eternal significance. “Maybe I should repair this relationship” becomes a spiritually serious thought.

Death

  1. Death does not get the final word.

  2. Funerals become temporary separations rather than absolute endings.

  3. Grief can coexist with hope.

  4. The dead remain members of one's moral community.

  5. You can rehearse mortality without surrendering to nihilism.

  6. Your own death becomes imaginable. Temple symbolism repeatedly situates mortal life within creation, fall, redemption, and return.

  7. You can practice letting go. Ritual trains surrender.

Moral agency

  1. Choices matter.

  2. Promises matter.

  3. Keeping promises changes the person keeping them.

  4. Freedom isn't merely doing whatever I want. Freedom can mean acquiring the capacity to become what one has chosen.

  5. Self-restraint can serve something larger than self-denial.

  6. Repentance means identity isn't permanently frozen by yesterday.

  7. Failure needn't be final.

  8. Moral development can continue throughout life.

  9. Power should be connected with responsibility.

Attention

This category is enormous.

  1. The temple directs attention toward things modern life encourages us to forget: death, ancestors, promises, creation, marriage, sacrifice, God, eternity.

  2. Clothing can become mnemonic. Something worn can trigger remembrance.

  3. Architecture can teach. Space itself becomes part of the story.

  4. Repeated words deepen memory. Repetition creates mental grooves.

  5. Symbols permit inexhaustible interpretation. You can encounter the same ritual hundreds of times and notice something different.

  6. Silence becomes meaningful rather than empty.

  7. Beauty receives spiritual significance.

  8. Attention itself becomes devotional.

  9. Ordinary life can become saturated with reminders of sacred things.

Sociology

  1. The billionaire and janitor can wear essentially the same temple clothing. Ordinary status markers are deliberately muted.

  2. Occupational hierarchy temporarily disappears.

  3. People perform ritual cooperatively.

  4. Strangers call one another brother and sister.

  5. A community repeatedly rehearses shared commitments.

  6. Individual identity becomes nested inside communal identity.

  7. Generations become symbolically joined into one community.

  8. A worldwide church performs substantially recognizable rituals across national boundaries.

Anthropology

  1. Rites of passage tell participants that life has structure.

  2. Sacred clothing marks transition between ordinary and sacred worlds.

  3. Sacred space creates psychological boundaries.

  4. Ritual repetition makes abstract ideas bodily. You don't merely think covenant; you physically participate in covenant-making.

  5. Narrative becomes enacted rather than merely read.

  6. The participant enters the story. Creation isn't simply something that happened to Adam and Eve; the worshipper symbolically locates himself or herself inside the human journey.

  7. Ritual compresses enormous ideas into memorable actions.

  8. Community memory can survive individuals because ritual carries it forward.

Philosophy

  1. Reality may contain more than what is immediately visible.

  2. Meaning can precede complete understanding.

  3. Knowledge can involve participation as well as observation. Some things are understood differently from inside a practice.

  4. Commitment can precede certainty.

  5. A human being can organize life around ultimate rather than immediate goods.

  6. Sacrifice reveals what one actually values.

  7. Identity can be chosen and practiced rather than merely discovered.

  8. The good life might involve becoming rather than acquiring.

Practical psychology

  1. I have somewhere to go when life becomes chaotic.

  2. I have a practiced ritual for slowing down.

  3. I repeatedly remember my highest commitments.

  4. I periodically escape advertising and consumption.

  5. I rehearse gratitude.

  6. I rehearse humility.

  7. I rehearse fidelity.

  8. I rehearse sacrifice.

  9. I rehearse returning home.

And there's an especially interesting one:

  1. I practice seeing my ordinary life as sacred.

That could have enormous consequences.

The treasures send me back into mortality.

And then something has to happen.

Remembering mercy should produce mercy.

Remembering ancestors should affect descendants.

Covenant should become conduct.

Symbol should become service.

Rehearsal should become performance.

Temple should become life.

So this little mnemonic isn't really about escaping into an imaginary temple.

It is about visiting the temple in memory so that I can return more deliberately to the life actually in front of me.

And perhaps that makes this tiny Portkey a miniature version of the entire experiment:

I encounter a symbol.

I remember.

I travel.

I receive.

I return.

And then I try to live differently.

For the rest of my life.
