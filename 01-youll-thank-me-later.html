YOU'LL THANK ME LATER! ### Bearing One Another's Burdens, Inheriting Zion, and Finding the Strength to Rest

The MORE Jesus = More Reality Experiment

By Saint Greg

1. ADRIAN MONK, A SUNDIAL, AND A VERY PECULIAR PROMISE

I love Adrian Monk.

If you've watched the television series Monk, you know that Adrian does some peculiar things.

He straightens objects. He rearranges things. He notices tiny details that everybody else overlooks. Sometimes he becomes so preoccupied with correcting something that the people around him can scarcely believe what they're witnessing.

In one episode, Mr. Monk and the Captain's Wife, Monk notices that a woman's sundial is wrong.

So he starts fixing it.

Now, this woman hasn't invited Monk into her yard to repair her sundial. She doesn't particularly appreciate his unsolicited assistance.

In fact, she's rather irritated.

But Monk corrects the sundial anyway.

And as he finishes, he announces:

"YOU'LL THANK ME LATER!"

Ha!

I love that!

There's something wonderfully ridiculous about Monk's confidence that somebody will eventually appreciate a service they didn't request and don't presently want.

And sometimes Monk is right.

But here's an even better discovery.

In another episode, Mr. Monk and Little Monk, we see young Adrian's mother preparing him a meticulously organized lunch, cut into ten little squares.

Young Adrian is embarrassed.

His mother, however, believes she's helping him.

And she tells him:

"You'll thank me later."

Oh, that's wonderful!

Monk inherited the phrase from Mom!

And now, decades later, Greg Muller is inheriting it from Monk.

Because I've been doing some peculiar things myself.

I'm making Jesus Pillows.

I'm redeeming rock songs.

I'm creating Jesus TV.

I'm running 26.2-foot Nano Marathons.

I'm writing thought pieces, building religious art collages, making little altars, and constructing an elaborate world I call My Jesus-Greg WORLD.

And sometimes I imagine somebody looking over my shoulder and wondering:

What on earth is Greg doing?

So I turn toward the camera on Jesus TV, throw my arm into the air, and announce:

"YOU'LL THANK ME LATER!"

Ha!

Maybe you will.

Maybe you won't.

But underneath the joke is something very serious.

Because I have a problem I've been trying to resolve with Jesus.

And I believe He's been teaching me how to rest.

2. JESUS, HOW MUCH DO YOU NEED OUT OF ME?

Here's my problem.

Jesus gives me things.

At least, that's how I understand what's been happening in my life.

Ideas.

Images.

Songs.

Connections.

Ways of seeing ordinary things that fill me with delight.

And when I receive something wonderful, I want to share it.

I want other people to see what I'm seeing.

I want them to experience what I'm experiencing.

I want them to discover the possibility that Jesus Christ is far more active, imaginative, playful, and involved in ordinary life than they might have supposed.

And so I ask:

"Jesus, how much do You need out of me?

"You've given me all these things.

"Are You expecting me to share them with other people?"

And in my understanding, the answer is:

"Yes, Greg. Share them with everybody."

After all, Jesus instructed His disciples to go into all the world and preach the gospel to every creature (Mark 16:15).

And Alma expressed something I recognize immediately:

"O that I were an angel" (Alma 29:1).

Oh, yes!

I understand that desire!

I want to announce the good news!

I want everybody to hear!

But then comes my problem.

They don't necessarily want what I'm offering.

One of my little redeemed songs expresses the dilemma:

*Jesus gives me things to share with you,
But Jesus doesn't make you want them too.*

There's the problem!

I can receive something I consider extraordinary.

I can offer it.

I can explain it.

I can make it beautiful.

I can put it on a website.

I can send somebody a link.

And they can say:

"No, thanks."

Or perhaps they won't say anything at all.

So what am I supposed to do?

Keep trying harder?

Become a more persuasive salesman?

Feel guilty because I'm not reaching enough people?

And while we're being candid, here's another question:

Jesus, what thanks do I get?

I don't apologize for wanting my contributions to matter.

I don't apologize for wanting people to receive the gifts I'm offering.

And I don't apologize for occasionally wanting somebody to say:

"Hey, Greg! Thank you!"

But I do want to understand what Jesus expects of me.

Because I want to contribute to Zion.

And I also want to rest.

3. BEAR ONE ANOTHER'S BURDENS

This brings me to one of the most beautiful passages in the Book of Mormon.

In Mosiah 18:8–9, Alma describes people willing to enter into a covenant with God.

They are:

"Willing to bear one another's burdens, that they may be light."

And they are willing to mourn with those who mourn, comfort those who stand in need of comfort, and stand as witnesses of God.

This is Zion material!

This is the kind of people I want to belong to.

People who care about one another.

People who contribute to one another's lives.

People who make burdens lighter.

People who don't simply look out for themselves.

And I want to be one of those people.

I want my little gifts to make somebody's burden lighter.

Maybe a redeemed rock song will help somebody remember Jesus.

Maybe a Jesus Pillow will help somebody pray.

Maybe Jesus TV will help somebody recognize Christ in an ordinary circumstance.

Maybe a thought piece will help somebody see something they hadn't previously considered.

Maybe a Nano Marathon will help somebody discover that a meaningful accomplishment doesn't have to be enormous.

And maybe none of those things will help a particular person.

That's okay.

But I want to offer them.

Because I want to bear one another's burdens.

And yet, as I've pondered this scripture, I believe Jesus has been teaching me something I hadn't adequately understood.

Something that changes the entire enterprise.

Before I become too preoccupied with bearing everybody else's burdens, perhaps I need to recognize how extensively my own burdens have already been borne.

4. GREG, YOU'RE STANDING ON THE SHOULDERS OF GIANTS

I didn't arrive on Earth in 1963 and start building civilization from scratch.

Good grief!

Imagine if I had to do that!

I'd have to invent language.

Agriculture.

Mathematics.

Medicine.

Electricity.

The printing press.

Automobiles.

Television.

Computers.

And rock and roll!

I'd never get around to making a Jesus Pillow.

But I didn't have to invent any of those things.

I inherited them.

I was born into a world that countless people had already been constructing.

Some of them lived thousands of years ago.

Some lived centuries ago.

Some were working the year before I was born.

And some were working last week.

Millions upon millions of people have been contributing to the life I now possess.

Some knowingly.

Some unknowingly.

Some deliberately trying to help future generations.

Others simply doing their jobs, pursuing their interests, raising their families, or making something they thought was beautiful.

And I have inherited the benefits.

My burdens have already been borne by people who never knew my name.

Think about that!

Before I was born, people were working to make my life possible.

And even today, people I will never meet are contributing to the conditions that make my life easier, safer, richer, more interesting, and more beautiful.

So when Alma invites me to bear one another's burdens, perhaps I should begin with a little humility.

I'm not arriving as some self-sufficient benefactor, generously descending from the heavens to rescue everybody else.

I'm arriving as somebody who has already received an almost incomprehensible inheritance.

I'm standing on the shoulders of giants.

And ordinary people.

Millions of ordinary people.

And now, with gratitude, I get to contribute something myself.

5. MORONI'S EXTRAORDINARY ASSIGNMENT

Moroni 10:3 invites us to remember how merciful the Lord has been to the children of men, from the creation of Adam down to the time we receive these things, and to ponder that history in our hearts.

From Adam!

All the way down to Greg Muller!

That's quite an assignment.

And I've been wondering whether I've understood how extensive it really is.

What if remembering the mercy of God includes remembering the countless people whose contributions have become my inheritance?

What if I need to recognize not only prophets and apostles, but inventors, philosophers, musicians, farmers, teachers, mothers, builders, and strangers?

What if God's mercy has reached me through people who didn't know they were contributing to my life?

And what if many of those people were being wrought upon by the Spirit of God?

Perhaps without understanding what was happening?

Perhaps without knowing what their contributions would eventually become?

Perhaps some wouldn't even have wanted to participate if they'd known how their work would eventually be used!

Now, I cannot demonstrate that every useful contribution was directly inspired by Jesus.

And I don't want to pretend I know the hidden divine explanation for every event in history.

But my scriptures invite me to consider a world in which God works through people in ways they don't always recognize.

And I want to explore that possibility.

Because I suspect I've been overlooking an enormous amount of my inheritance.

6. CHRISTOPHER COLUMBUS: YOU'LL THANK ME LATER!

Consider Christopher Columbus.

In 1 Nephi 13:12, Nephi describes a man among the Gentiles who is wrought upon by the Spirit of God and crosses the waters.

Latter-day Saints have traditionally associated that passage with Columbus.

And I take that interpretation seriously.

Columbus didn't simply wake up one morning and create the historical circumstances that eventually made my life in America possible.

He was one participant in an enormous process involving countless people, cultures, voyages, ideas, conflicts, and developments.

And the Americas were already inhabited by peoples with their own civilizations, histories, and inheritances. European colonization also brought devastating injustices and suffering.

Those realities matter.

Acknowledging a contribution doesn't require pretending that a contributor was perfect, or that everything resulting from his actions was good.

But in my reading of Nephi, something else matters, too.

He was wrought upon.

And 1 Nephi 13:13 describes the Spirit of God working upon others who crossed the waters.

That fascinates me.

Because these people weren't Latter-day Saints.

The modern Church hadn't been organized yet.

And yet, in this scriptural account, God was working upon people who would influence the future.

When I was born in 1963, I inherited American citizenship, constitutional protections, democratic institutions, and opportunities that had been developed through centuries of human effort.

That inheritance has imperfections, and its benefits have not always been equally enjoyed.

But I am grateful for what I've received.

And I can recognize Columbus as one contributor without imagining that he was the only contributor.

A national holiday can help us remember a particular contribution, even while we continue examining the fuller history.

So, Christopher Columbus:

Thank you for your contribution.

And thank You, Jesus, for the possibility that You were working upon him.

Perhaps Columbus couldn't have imagined that a fellow in Utah would someday be reflecting on his voyage as part of a lesson about Zion.

But here I am.

A few centuries later.

Thanking him.

7. THE GREEK PHILOSOPHERS DIDN'T KNOW ABOUT GREG

Consider ancient Greek philosophers and political thinkers.

They wrestled with questions of justice, citizenship, government, and human flourishing.

They weren't trying to establish the United States of America.

They didn't know about Greg Muller.

They didn't know about Utah.

They didn't know about Zion Coalition.

But elements of their intellectual inheritance eventually contributed, along with Roman, English, Enlightenment, colonial, and other influences, to political ideas and institutions that shaped the United States.

And I inherited benefits from that history.

That's the pattern!

A person can contribute to an inheritance without knowing who will eventually receive it.

The contribution can travel across centuries.

Across cultures.

Across religious boundaries.

Across generations.

And the eventual beneficiary may discover uses that the original contributor never imagined.

Which brings me to Axl Rose.

8. AXL ROSE DAY

I love Axl Rose.

Well, more precisely, I love some of the extraordinary musical gifts that Axl Rose and his collaborators have given the world.

And one of those gifts is November Rain.

Now, Axl didn't write that song for Greg Muller.

He didn't know that someday I would be sitting here imagining how to redeem it into a Jesus worship device.

He wasn't trying to build Zion Coalition.

And I have no reason to suppose he would endorse my religious interpretation of his work.

But here's what happened.

When I began creating my redeemed version, I discovered that much of the difficult creative work had already been done.

The musical structure was there.

The emotional power was there.

The artistic inheritance was there.

Axl and his collaborators had already done extraordinary work.

And now I could receive it and make my own contribution.

I could transform my experience of the song into something that reminded me of Jesus.

And in my understanding, Jesus was helping me see that possibility.

So I could identify three participants in my story:

Jesus.

Axl.

Greg.

Not equal participants.

Not necessarily conscious collaborators.

But contributors to something that now helps me worship.

And I found myself wanting to throw my arm into the air and shout:

"THANK YOU, AXL ROSE!"

And:

"THANK YOU, JESUS, FOR AXL ROSE!"

Now here's where Zion Coalition enters the picture.

If I were a country, perhaps we'd have Axl Rose Day!

Ha!

Why not?

We have days to commemorate people whose contributions shaped our inheritance.

Why shouldn't my little world celebrate a musician whose work has become part of my spiritual inheritance?

Not because Axl is perfect.

Not because he belongs to my church.

Not because he intended to contribute to Zion.

But because I can recognize a contribution.

And I can thank him.

And perhaps that's something a Zion people ought to become particularly good at doing.

9. GOD'S WORK IS BIGGER THAN MY TRIBE

Now, there's a problem.

It's hard to keep everything in mind at once.

Just look at this essay!

Adrian Monk.

Christopher Columbus.

Joseph Smith.

Axl Rose.

Greek philosophers.

Philo Farnsworth.

Mom.

Jesus.

Zion.

Nano Marathons.

Good grief!

No wonder people specialize.

No wonder organizations develop particular missions.

A church emphasizes its religious inheritance.

A historical society emphasizes a particular history.

A family remembers its ancestors.

A charitable organization concentrates on the people it serves.

And that's understandable.

I don't fault people for concentrating on their particular part of the elephant.

But I don't want to mistake my part of the elephant for the whole elephant.

I'm a Latter-day Saint.

I treasure the Book of Mormon, the Restoration, Joseph Smith, temple worship, and the particular religious inheritance I've received.

But if I concentrate exclusively on Latter-day Saint contributions, I may overlook the contributions of others.

And if I concentrate exclusively on Christian contributions, I may overlook even more.

God works with Jews and Gentiles. Latter-day Saints and non-Latter-day Saints. Christians and non-Christians.

At least, that's the expansive possibility I see in my scriptures.

And I want to become better at recognizing it.

Within my particular religious framework, Axl Rose might not be an obvious candidate for contributing to Zion.

But look!

I can see how his music has contributed to the work I'm doing.

And I believe Jesus has been helping me see it.

Perhaps that's part of the point of this whole experiment.

I don't want the boundaries of my religious tribe—or the limits of my own attention—to become the boundaries of where I'm willing to recognize Jesus at work.

10. ZION COALITION: RECOGNIZING THE CONTRIBUTORS

This is one of the reasons I've been preparing Zion Coalition.

Most organizations begin by recruiting members.

They invite people to join, agree to a mission, and then contribute.

But I'm experimenting with a different sequence.

What if we begin by recognizing contributions that have already been made?

What if we identify people who have helped build the inheritance of Zion, whether they knew it or not?

What if we write them letters?

What if we say:

"Dear Axl,

"You may not know this, but your work has become part of my spiritual inheritance.

"I've received something valuable from you.

"And I want you to know that I see it.

"Thank you."

The letter doesn't require Axl to accept my theology.

It doesn't require him to join an organization.

It doesn't require him to endorse my use of his music.

It's an acknowledgment.

And perhaps an invitation.

I can recognize someone as a contributor to Zion within my own understanding without pretending they've consented to formal membership.

And this recognition can extend far beyond Axl.

To Joseph Smith.

To Philo Farnsworth.

To my mother.

To Greek philosophers.

To the people who built the roads.

To people who preserved knowledge.

To people who developed technologies.

To people who made sacrifices I may never fully understand.

And to people who contributed something wonderful without ever imagining what it might become.

Zion Coalition, in this sense, is not merely gathering people who intend to build Zion. It is learning to recognize and honor people whose gifts may already be helping build it.

And perhaps the recognition itself can become a gift.

Because somebody might finally hear:

"I see what you've contributed.

"And I'm grateful."

11. MOM: YOU'LL THANK ME LATER

Now let's return to Monk's mother.

Because Mom may be one of the most important contributors of all.

Children often don't understand how extensively their mothers have been bearing their burdens.

They see the rules.

The interruptions.

The discipline.

The inconvenient instructions.

They don't necessarily see the worry.

The sacrifice.

The exhaustion.

The years of work.

The countless little decisions made for their benefit.

And Mom may say:

"You'll thank me later."

Sometimes the child finally understands at thirty-eight.

Sometimes at sixty-two.

Sometimes never.

But the contribution was made regardless.

The burden was borne.

And the child's inability to appreciate it didn't erase its value.

I wonder how many other people I've treated like that.

How many gifts have I received without recognizing the giver?

How many contributions have I overlooked because they didn't arrive in a form I expected?

And how often have I treated Jesus that way?

12. CHARITY FOR JESUS

We talk about Jesus having charity for us.

We talk about having charity for our neighbors.

But what about having charity for Jesus?

What if I don't understand what He's doing?

What if I don't recognize His gifts?

What if I'm like a child who doesn't yet appreciate Mom's sacrifices?

Perhaps I can extend Jesus the benefit of the doubt.

Perhaps I can say:

"Jesus, I don't understand everything You're doing.

"But I want to remain open to Your goodness.

"I want to learn to recognize Your gifts.

"And I want to thank You for what I can already see."

This doesn't mean pretending that every event is good.

It doesn't mean claiming certainty about every circumstance.

It means being willing to consider that my present understanding may be incomplete.

That the gift may be larger than I realize.

That the giver may be doing something I don't yet comprehend.

And perhaps someday I'll look back and say:

"Jesus, NOW I see!"

And I'll throw my arm into the air.

"THANK YOU, JESUS!"

13. PHILO FARNSWORTH AND THE GIFTS NOBODY YET KNOWS HOW TO RECEIVE

Consider Philo Farnsworth.

He helped pioneer electronic television.

When he was developing that technology, most people couldn't simply turn on a television and discover its possibilities.

The inheritance wasn't yet available to them in the way it would eventually become available.

And even Farnsworth later expressed concerns about some of the uses to which television was being put.

But look what happened.

Television became part of the inheritance of generations.

And now I'm using the concept of television as a central metaphor for my relationship with Jesus.

Jesus TV!

What I See on Jesus TV!

My Head Makes Movies!

Philo didn't know about Greg Muller.

He didn't know what I would eventually do with the technology he helped pioneer.

And I don't know whether he would have particularly appreciated my peculiar Jesus TV experiments.

But I can thank him anyway.

"THANK YOU, PHILO FARNSWORTH!"

And I can recognize that his contribution became useful to somebody he never met.

That's important to me.

Because I don't know who might someday benefit from the things I'm making.

And perhaps they can't appreciate them yet.

Perhaps they don't have a use for them.

Perhaps they haven't developed an appetite for them.

Perhaps the particular circumstances in which my little art piece would become meaningful haven't arrived.

And perhaps they never will.

But I don't need to settle that question today.

14. THE SECOND GIFT: WANTING THE GIFT

There's something else Jesus has been teaching me.

Receiving a gift isn't merely a matter of having the gift available.

The recipient also needs some desire for it.

Some appetite.

Some capacity to recognize its value.

A person who doesn't want a particular gift may find it useless, irritating, or burdensome.

Even if another person treasures it.

That's the problem with my redeemed rock songs.

I can make them.

I can share them.

But I can't make somebody want them.

And maybe I shouldn't try.

Jesus Himself invited people to come unto Him, and not everybody accepted the invitation.

Alma longed to proclaim repentance with the voice of an angel, yet he also came to recognize the need to be content with the portion allotted to him.

Perhaps I can learn something from that.

I can offer what I've received.

I can make it available.

I can invite people to consider it.

But I don't have to manufacture their desire.

The gift is mine to offer. The receiving is theirs to choose.

And that distinction is beginning to give me rest.

Now comes one of my favorite little exchanges in My Jesus-Greg WORLD.

I've spent much of my life wanting to be around cool, powerful people doing cool, powerful things.

Inventors.

Artists.

Thinkers.

Builders.

People changing the world.

And in what I understand as a playful conversation with Jesus, He asks me something like this:

"Greg, what about My Father?

"What about Me?

"What about the Holy Ghost?

"What about angels?

"Are we not good enough for Greg????"

Oh!

Well!

When You put it THAT way!

Perhaps I've been overlooking the most extraordinary company imaginable.

Perhaps I've been searching for greatness while failing to recognize the greatness already offered to me.

And perhaps my friend Lyman can be my Neal A. Maxwell.

Perhaps I don't need to be surrounded by famous people to be surrounded by extraordinary people.

Perhaps the King Himself wants to be my Friend.

And perhaps the work of Zion is already available to people like Lyman and Greg.

Right here.

With the gifts we've already received.

16. MY INN AND MY EXPECTED MESSIAH

The people who awaited the Messiah had expectations.

And when Jesus arrived, He didn't necessarily resemble the Messiah they expected.

A manger?

A carpenter?

A man from Nazareth?

Really?

And now I wonder whether I'm doing something similar.

I want Jesus to do extraordinary things.

But what if He is doing extraordinary things through ordinary circumstances?

What if He's working through a rock song?

Through my mother?

Through Lyman?

Through a tiny marathon?

Through some ridiculous little thing Greg is making in his workshop?

Am I willing to make room for that Jesus?

Or am I going to tell Him there's no room in my inn because I'm waiting for a more impressive Messiah?

And am I going to overlook somebody else's contribution because that person doesn't belong to the religious or cultural community I expected?

17. BE STRONG ENOUGH TO REST

And now I come to what may be the highest understanding of this entire experiment.

Rest.

Jesus says:

"Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." (Matthew 11:28)

And in My Jesus-Greg WORLD, I hear Him saying something like this:

"Greg, I need you to understand something.

"You are not starting from nothing.

"I've had people working on your inheritance for centuries.

"For decades.

"For months.

"For weeks.

"Even yesterday.

"Millions of people have been contributing to the life you now possess.

"Most of them didn't know your name.

"Many didn't even know what their contributions would eventually become.

"Some might not have wanted to participate if they'd known how their gifts would someday be used.

"But Greg, look what you've inherited!

"You don't have to do all that work yourself.

"You couldn't do all that work yourself!

"I need you to be strong enough to rest.

"Receive your inheritance.

"And then become a good steward of it."

Oh!

So perhaps this is what I've been learning.

I don't have to compose November Rain. Axl Rose and his collaborators have already done that work.

I don't have to invent television. Philo Farnsworth and others have already helped give me that inheritance.

I don't have to create a civilization from nothing. Generations of people have contributed to the society in which I live.

I don't have to invent scripture, language, music, philosophy, or every instrument I use to worship.

I can receive.

I can recognize.

I can thank.

And then I can do something with what I've inherited.

Not because I'm lazy.

Because inheriting is not lazy work. Inheriting makes me a steward.

And stewardship is a sacred responsibility.

Perhaps that's why Jesus wants me to see the contributions of so many people.

Not merely so I'll become more grateful.

But so I'll finally understand that I'm not carrying the whole world on my shoulders.

The world has been carrying me.

And, in my faith, Jesus has been carrying me through the work of countless other people.

Some of them Latter-day Saints.

Some not.

Some Christians.

Some not.

Some who knew what they were doing.

Many who didn't.

And perhaps some who would be astonished to discover what their contributions have become.

This is why I want Zion Coalition to recognize them.

This is why I want to send letters.

This is why I want to celebrate Axl Rose Day.

And this is why I want to throw my arm into the air and shout:

"THANK YOU, JESUS!"

"THANK YOU, AXL!"

"THANK YOU, MOM!"

Because I am beginning to recognize how much I've received.

And maybe the first great lesson in learning to bear one another's burdens is discovering that I have never been bearing mine alone.

18. YOU'LL THANK ME LATER!

Now let's return to Adrian Monk.

Because I don't want anybody to miss the joke.

Sometimes I look at what I'm doing in My Jesus-Greg WORLD and I have to laugh.

I'm making Jesus Pillows.

I'm redeeming rock songs.

I'm creating Jesus TV.

I'm running 26.2-foot marathons.

I'm building an entire imaginative world around the possibility that Jesus Christ is alive, active, and inviting me to see Him everywhere.

And sometimes I imagine somebody looking over my shoulder, wondering what on earth Greg Muller is doing.

And I turn toward the camera on Jesus TV and announce:

"YOU'LL THANK ME LATER!"

Ha!

Maybe you will!

Maybe you won't!

Maybe ten thousand people will inherit something useful from this work.

Maybe ten.

Maybe two.

I don't know.

And here's the wonderful thing:

I don't have to know.

I'm going to create as though lots of people might someday benefit, because I don't know who those people are.

And if it turns out that only one or two people receive something valuable, that's fine with me.

Jesus and I can still have a wonderful time making it.

Because I've already inherited an extraordinary amount from people who didn't know me, didn't know what I would do with their gifts, and couldn't possibly have required my gratitude in advance.

Axl Rose didn't know Greg Muller would someday turn November Rain into a Jesus worship device.

Philo Farnsworth didn't know Greg Muller would someday create Jesus TV.

My mother didn't know all the ways I would eventually understand and appreciate her contributions.

And I don't know what somebody might someday do with one of my little art pieces.

That's part of the adventure.

I'm not promising that my art will change the world.

I'm not demanding that anybody recognize my genius.

I'm simply enjoying the possibility that Jesus can do something useful with my contributions, just as I believe He has done with the contributions of countless other people.

And I'm willing to leave the eventual reception of those gifts in other people's hands.

So when you see me doing something strange, please remember Adrian Monk.

Maybe I'm arranging pencils.

Maybe I'm building Zion.

Maybe I'm doing a little of both.

YOU'LL THANK ME LATER!

And if you don't?

Well, Jesus is still my Friend.

And I still had a wonderful time making it.

19. BEFORE WE BEAR ONE ANOTHER'S BURDENS

Now let's return one final time to Mosiah 18.

Because I think this is where the entire experiment comes together.

Alma invites us to become people willing to bear one another's burdens.

And I want to say yes.

Yes, Jesus!

I want to help!

I want to contribute!

I want to share what You've given me!

But perhaps before I rush off to carry everybody else's burdens, I need to recognize something.

My burdens have already been carried by countless others.

By Mom.

By Axl Rose.

By Philo Farnsworth.

By Joseph Smith.

By Christopher Columbus.

By philosophers and inventors and builders and farmers and teachers.

By people whose names I know.

By people whose names I will never know.

By people who lived centuries before I was born.

By people who worked last week.

By people who knew they were helping.

By people who had no idea.

And, in my understanding of the scriptures, perhaps many of them were being wrought upon by the Spirit of God.

So I want to learn to recognize them.

I want to thank them.

I want to acknowledge their contributions.

I want to become a better inheritor.

And then I want to become a better steward.

Because I don't want merely to consume my inheritance.

I want to contribute something to the inheritance of others.

That's what Zion Coalition is preparing to do.

To recognize the contributors.

To celebrate the gifts.

To make visible the enormous network of burden-bearing that has made our lives possible.

And then to participate in it ourselves.

Not anxiously.

Not desperately.

Not because we have to prove our worth.

But gratefully.

Generously.

And, whenever possible, joyfully.

We do not enter the work of bearing one another's burdens as self-sufficient benefactors. We enter as grateful inheritors of burdens already borne for us by countless others.

And now, because we've inherited so much, we can contribute what we can.

Maybe somebody will thank us later.

Maybe they won't.

But the contribution can still be worth making.

"Jesus, how much do You need out of me?

"How much am I supposed to share?

"And what if nobody wants what I'm giving?"

And in my sacred imagination, Jesus responds:

"Greg, remember Mom.

"Remember Philo Farnsworth.

"Remember Joseph Smith.

"Remember Christopher Columbus.

"Remember Axl Rose.

"Look how much you've inherited from people who couldn't make you appreciate their gifts when they gave them.

"And now you're thanking them.

"Greg, you've received an enormous inheritance.
