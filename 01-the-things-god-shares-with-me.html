First, tune in: What is the sound?

When an ordinary object becomes a VeilKey—a carrot, a pillow—the first invitation is not merely to look at it. It is to listen. What is the sound? The Jesus Carrot's sound is this song: a personal redemption of Bob Dylan's Make You Feel My Love, widely known through Adele and other singers.

My carrot is a pretend microphone that takes me, by imagination, to the formal microphone at the church podium. The song explains why that microphone matters to me: I have spent my life longing to share discoveries and what I understand as news from Jesus, sometimes without noticing that others did not want to hear. The song holds both the ache of that experience and the freedom of learning to offer without forcing.

Why this song belongs to the carrot

The carrot is funny, humble, and informal; the church microphone is formal, amplified, and institutionally entrusted. Both are microphones in my personal theater, but only one asks for a room's attention. The carrot lets me remember the formal microphone without claiming it.

The melody is melancholy for a reason. My desire to share has been powerful, and giving up the expectation of an audience has not always been painless. But the song's turning point is its final verse: the hope is not that everyone listens to me; it is that others discover what God might have to share with them.

What I am practicing: The fire to discover and create may remain. I can make the work, extend an invitation, and let other people freely decide whether to listen. The microphone need not be seized. That is one way I am learning rest.

The sound opens the next door

This is the first sound exhibit in a museum still being assembled. The song leads back to the podium, to my history of speaking at church, to the INNkeepers and the manger, and outward to the other stories and people that will eventually be linked here.
