THE GIFT OF FASCINATION

Catch the Mystery. Get On with the Fascination.

A Jesus-Greg WORLD Thought Piece

The MORE Jesus = More Reality Experiment

A PRELUDE: A BIBLE! A BIBLE! WE HAVE A BIBLE!

Before you read this thought piece, you should know something about where I intend to put it.

Straight into my Bible.

Not necessarily the leather-bound one sitting on the shelf. My Bible has become something much larger than a bound collection of ancient writings. It has become a metaphor for the growing collection of things through which I believe Jesus Christ is teaching me.

Actually, I suspect the Bible has always been something like that: a collection of testimonies, encounters, revelations, and sacred writings that somebody, somewhere, decided were worth preserving.

Nephi had his plates. Joseph Smith had his revelations. And Greg Muller has his rather peculiar collection of Jesus-themed thought pieces, songs, pictures, pillows, and other assorted sacred oddities.

Now, before anybody reaches for the heresy alarm, I'm not claiming to be Nephi or Joseph Smith. I'm not claiming that my writings carry their authority, or that everyone else ought to put them in their Bible.

I'm saying something both simpler and, perhaps, more outrageous.

My best guess is that Jesus Christ is not dead. Nor does He sleep.

I believe He still speaks. I believe He still teaches. I believe He still gives people things worth writing down. And I believe that those who seek Him can receive new scripture—personal sacred writings that become part of their own unfolding record of life with Christ.

I receive thought pieces like this as what I believe may be gifts from Jesus. I try to listen, write, examine, and preserve them. I don't pretend that my hearing is infallible. My fingerprints are all over these pages. So are my misunderstandings, my imagination, my sense of humor, and probably a few theological banana peels.

But I don't think Jesus requires me to become an infallible stenographer before I can begin writing down what I believe He is teaching me.

So yes, I believe in an open canon—at least in the sense that my personal collection of sacred writings remains open to whatever Jesus may yet teach me. That's different from claiming authority to enlarge the Church's official canon.

And I invite other people to consider the possibility that their own Bibles may still have blank pages.

Not pages for replacing the ancient witnesses, but pages for recording their own encounters with the living Christ.

After all, if Jesus really is alive, why should the conversation have ended?

And if He has something to say to me today, why would I refuse to write it down simply because I already own a perfectly good King James Version?

A Bible! A Bible! We have a Bible!

Yes, indeed.

And perhaps, by the grace of a living Christ, there is still more to write.

Which brings me to this morning's installment.

The Gift of Fascination.

I. Something Happened to Me This Morning

I have been wrought upon.

That's the best language I have for what happened this morning. Not necessarily a revelation in the spectacular sense. No angel appeared. No heavenly messenger handed me a golden book. Nothing particularly impressive happened to an outside observer.

But something happened to my wanting.

I found myself thinking about President Russell M. Nelson's prophecy:

“In coming days, we will see the greatest manifestations of the Savior's power that the world has ever seen.”

And his later invitation:

“I pray that we will not miss the majesty of this moment!”

And suddenly I felt myself wanting something that I have not always wanted very much.

I wanted to watch.

I wanted to see what Jesus Christ is going to do.

More importantly, I wanted to care about what Jesus Christ is going to do.

And that last realization stopped me.

What if the great gift Jesus is preparing to give me isn't the ability to witness His mighty works?

What if the first great gift is the desire to witness them?

What if Jesus is giving me the gift of fascination?

II. Am I Just Rubbernecking?

Now, upon deep human reflection, I have to admit that there is something potentially thin about all this.

Am I just a religious rubbernecker?

Am I suddenly interested in Jesus because I believe He's about to do some really cool things?

Am I like somebody driving past a spectacular accident, slowing down to see what happened?

Or like a person who hears that the greatest show in human history is about to begin and thinks, Well, I certainly don't want to miss THAT!

Is that discipleship?

Is that love?

Or am I just bored with Babylon?

Because, frankly, Babylon can get pretty boring.

Not because Babylon lacks entertainment. Quite the opposite. Babylon is extraordinarily good at entertaining us.

But there is something exhausting about an endless parade of manufactured spectacles, each one demanding my attention, each one promising to be more interesting than the last.

I've spent much of my life being trained not to be impressed.

Don't be gullible. Don't get carried away. Don't believe weird things. Don't fall for conspiracies. Don't let yourself become fascinated by things that sensible people have already learned to dismiss.

And much of that training has been useful.

But what if some of it has also trained me not to care about things that deserve my attention?

What if I've become so practiced at protecting myself from false wonders that I've also become resistant to genuine wonder?

And what if Jesus is now inviting me to become impressed again?

Not indiscriminately.

Not foolishly.

But lovingly.

III. Catch the Mystery

Two passages from Rush's Limelight have been circling through my mind.

The first gives me a marvelous little instruction:

“Catch the mystery.”

And the second gives me another:

“Get on with the fascination.”

Now, I'm not claiming Rush wrote a hymn about my relationship with Jesus Christ.

But I am claiming that Jesus can use a rock song to remind me of Himself.

That's part of the Jesus-Greg WORLD experiment.

The song speaks to the tension between appearance and authenticity, between alienation and genuine relationship.

And suddenly I hear those tensions in my own religious life.

Am I trying to seem like a person who witnesses marvelous works?

Or am I becoming a person who genuinely wants to behold the works of Jesus Christ?

Those are different things.

One is performance.

The other might be transformation.

And the difference matters enormously.

Because I don't want to manufacture a religious persona who claims to see Jesus everywhere.

I want to become someone who actually loves looking for Him.

Someone who wants to find Him in people, places, things, and circumstances.

Someone whose fascination is not merely with the spectacular things Jesus might do, but with Jesus Himself.

Catch the mystery. Get on with the fascination.

Maybe that's an invitation I have been waiting sixty-two years to hear.

IV. Is Awe an Approved Religious Activity?

I think so.

Actually, I think awe is one of the oldest religious activities there is.

“Come and see the works of God,” says Psalm 66:5.

Not come and accomplish the works of God.

Not come and manage the works of God.

Not even come and explain the works of God.

Come and see.

“Be still, and know that I am God,” says Psalm 46:10.

There is something deeply religious about becoming still enough to behold.

Nephi's great vision in 1 Nephi 11 unfolds through invitations to look, behold, and understand.

Joseph Smith and Sidney Rigdon describe witnessing extraordinary heavenly things in Doctrine and Covenants 76.

The scriptures are full of watchers.

People who looked.

People who wondered.

People who were astonished.

People who sometimes fell on their faces because what they were seeing was more than they knew how to comprehend.

And then there is Mary, who kept things and pondered them in her heart.

Perhaps pondering is another kind of watching.

Perhaps awe is not a preliminary distraction from worship.

Perhaps awe is one of worship's essential ingredients.

V. Mostly Watching

For quite some time now, I have been developing an understanding of my own assignment in the Jesus-Greg WORLD.

I suspect that I will spend much of the rest of my life mostly watching.

Not necessarily doing mighty works.

Not necessarily organizing spectacular religious movements.

Not necessarily becoming a great preacher, prophet, teacher, or miracle worker.

Mostly watching.

Watching Jesus.

Watching what I believe He is doing in the world.

Watching what He might be doing in ordinary people.

Watching for Him in strange places.

Watching for Him in things I once considered insignificant.

Watching for Him in a pillow, a mountain, a song, a dandelion, a stranger, a memory, a broken appliance, or a conversation.

And yes, watching for Him in the extraordinary things that President Nelson says are coming.

But here's the important part.

Mostly watching does not mean doing nothing.

Watching can require enormous attention.

Watching can require patience.

Watching can require humility.

Watching can require the willingness to be wrong about what I think I'm seeing.

Watching can require the courage to care about things that other people find ridiculous.

And watching can require love.

Perhaps especially love.

Because I have discovered that I pay attention differently to things I love.

I can look at something a hundred times without really seeing it.

Then I fall in love with it.

And suddenly I begin noticing things I never noticed before.

What if that is part of what Jesus is doing to me?

What if He is enlarging my capacity to notice Him by enlarging my capacity to love Him?

VI. The Gift Might Not Be Seeing

Alma 32:27 has become especially interesting to me:

“Even if ye can no more than desire to believe, let this desire work in you.”

There it is.

A desire.

Not certainty.

Not knowledge.

Not an extraordinary spiritual accomplishment.

Just the beginning of wanting.

Alma treats that beginning as something worth cultivating.

And I find myself wondering whether the principle might extend beyond believing.

What if I can no more than desire to care?

What if I want to become fascinated by the works of Jesus Christ, but find that much of me is still strangely indifferent?

What if I want to watch, but I have spent decades training myself to look away?

Could that desire itself be a beginning?

Could the desire to care be something Jesus can enlarge?

And might the enlargement itself be a gift?

Maybe the miracle isn't that I suddenly see more of Jesus. Maybe the miracle is that I increasingly want to see Him.

That's a different kind of miracle.

And perhaps a more foundational one.

Because what good would it do me to witness the greatest manifestations of Christ's power if I didn't care?

What if I watched something marvelous happen and shrugged?

What if I had eyes but had never developed the desire to see?

VII. Weird, Wild Things

Now we come to a particularly uncomfortable part of this experiment.

I already believe some pretty wild things.

I believe in golden plates.

Angels.

Ancient prophets.

A miraculous compass called the Liahona.

A resurrected Savior who can speak to human beings.

A God who can accomplish things that exceed my present understanding of reality.

So it would be rather strange for me to insist that Jesus must restrict His future activities to things I already find perfectly ordinary and respectable.

President Nelson says the greatest manifestations of the Savior's power are yet to be seen.

Well!

What exactly am I expecting?

More of the same?

Only things that comfortably fit my existing categories?

Only things that would receive immediate approval from respectable institutions?

Perhaps not.

But there is an important distinction here.

Being willing to consider extraordinary possibilities does not mean believing every extraordinary claim.

I don't need to abandon discernment in order to recover wonder.

I don't need to believe every conspiracy theory in order to take prophecy seriously.

I don't need to confuse my imagination with revelation.

I can wonder.

I can investigate.

I can pray.

I can test.

I can change my mind.

I can say What if? without immediately insisting Therefore, it is!

And perhaps Jesus can work with that.

Perhaps sacred imagination is not the enemy of careful discernment.

Perhaps the two belong together.

I want to be open enough to notice something unexpected and grounded enough not to mistake every unusual thing for a divine manifestation.

I want to become a better watcher.

Not merely a more excitable one.

VIII. The Viewer Is Being Changed

Here is where my Jesus TV metaphor becomes particularly interesting.

For years I have been constructing my own peculiar collection of Watch Rooms, galleries, pictures, songs, memories, and little artistic experiments.

The MORE Jesus = More Reality Experiment.

What I See on Jesus TV.

A world of reminders.

A world of invitations to look.

And perhaps I have been thinking about this all wrong.

Perhaps the most important thing happening on Jesus TV isn't what appears on the screen.

Perhaps the most important thing happening is what happens to the viewer.

The viewer begins to care.

The viewer becomes curious.

The viewer becomes patient.

The viewer becomes willing to be astonished.

The viewer starts looking for Jesus in people he previously ignored.

The viewer begins to recognize goodness in unexpected places.

The viewer becomes less interested in appearing spiritually impressive and more interested in genuinely loving what he beholds.

And perhaps, little by little, the viewer becomes more like the One he is watching.

That brings me to John 5:19, where Jesus describes doing what He sees the Father do.

Seeing and doing are not necessarily competitors.

Sometimes seeing prepares us to do.

Sometimes watching is how we learn to love.

And sometimes loving changes what we are capable of seeing.

IX. What If This Is One of His Mighty Works?

Now here is the thought that has been sneaking up on me all morning.

President Nelson says we are going to witness extraordinary manifestations of Jesus Christ's power.

And I have been wondering whether Jesus is preparing me to witness them.

But what if the preparation itself is already something marvelous?

What if Jesus takes an aging man who has spent much of his life being skeptical, distracted, entertained, bored, and unimpressed—

And gives him a new appetite?

An appetite for wonder.

An appetite for reality.

An appetite for noticing goodness.

An appetite for the works of God.

An appetite for Jesus Christ.

Would that count as a manifestation of His power?

I don't know how President Nelson would answer that question.

But I know how I am beginning to answer it.

I think the transformation of human desire can be a mighty work.

I think learning to care about what is good can be a mighty work.

I think recovering the ability to be astonished by the things of God can be a mighty work.

And I think the desire to witness Jesus Christ might itself be a gift worth receiving with gratitude.

Not because every feeling I have is necessarily a revelation.

Not because every fascinating possibility is true.

But because a growing love of Jesus Christ is worth cultivating.

And because the fruits of that love can be examined over time.

Am I becoming kinder?

More patient?

More truthful?

More willing to serve?

More capable of seeing Christ in the least of His brethren?

If so, perhaps something good is growing.

X. Don't Miss It

President Nelson's invitation has acquired a new meaning for me.

Don't miss it.

At first I heard that as a warning not to miss the great events.

Don't miss the miracles.

Don't miss the extraordinary manifestations.

Don't miss the astonishing things Jesus is going to do.

But now I hear another invitation inside it.

Don't miss the opportunity to become someone who cares.

Don't miss the chance to cultivate awe.

Don't miss the invitation to become fascinated by Jesus Christ.

Don't miss the ordinary people in whom His goodness might be reflected.

Don't miss the little things while waiting for the big things.

Don't miss the present because you're so preoccupied with the future.

And don't become so fascinated with the spectacle that you forget the Savior.

Because ultimately I don't want merely to be entertained by Jesus.

I want to love Him.

I want to know Him.

I want to become more like Him.

And if watching is one of the ways He teaches me to do that, then I want to watch.

Mostly watch, if that's what I'm called to do.

Watch with curiosity.

Watch with discernment.

Watch with gratitude.

Watch with an imagination open to surprise.

Watch with a heart increasingly willing to care.

XI. Get On with the Fascination

So here I am.

Sixty-two years old.

An old man dreaming dreams.

Still trying to figure out what Jesus is doing with me.

Still making strange little pieces of art.

Still building my Jesus-Greg WORLD.

Still finding Jesus in things that other people might find ridiculous.

Still trying to distinguish between my own imagination and whatever God might actually be showing me.

Still learning how to watch.

And this morning I find myself contemplating a gift I might not previously have recognized as a gift.

The gift of fascination.

Maybe I don't need to apologize for wanting to see the mighty works of Jesus Christ.

Maybe I don't need to be embarrassed by awe.

Maybe I don't need to treat wonder as an immature religious impulse that I must eventually outgrow.

Maybe fascination can mature.

Maybe fascination can become attention.

Attention can become understanding.

Understanding can become affection.

Affection can become love.

And love can become a way of life.

I don't know what extraordinary manifestations of Jesus Christ's power the coming days will bring.

I don't know how many I will recognize.

I don't know how many I will misunderstand.

I don't even know how many I will live to see.

But I find myself wanting to watch.

And, even more wonderfully, I find myself wanting to want to watch.

Perhaps that is where the gift begins.

Perhaps one of the greatest things Jesus can do for me is to make me increasingly interested in what He is doing.

Perhaps the first miracle is not on the screen.

Perhaps the first miracle is in the viewer.

Catch the mystery.

Get on with the fascination.

And whatever else happens—

Don't miss Jesus.

A POSTLUDE: ALL ABOARD THE OTHERWORLDLY EXPRESS

Yes. And I think I've identified something deeper than the gift of fascination itself.

I don't want to be a spectator of the greatest adventure in existence while somehow remaining outside it.

I want aboard the train.

Not necessarily as the engineer. Not necessarily as a prophet, apostle, or officially appointed conductor. I'd be delighted just to find a seat by the window.

But I want aboard.

And if the train is the ongoing, extraordinary work of the living Jesus Christ, I don't want to spend my one mortal life standing on the platform, debating whether the train is real, whether I'm qualified to board, or whether the ticket is sufficiently orthodox.

Alma 32:27 says: “Even if ye can no more than desire to believe, let this desire work in you.”

What if I can only desire to believe Jesus is doing something extraordinary? What if I can only desire to participate? What if I can only imagine my participation?

What if I construct a story—a sacred imaginative framework—in which my life and the works of Jesus Christ are connected? And what if that imaginative act itself changes how I live?

Perhaps I'm not merely asking whether the train is real. Perhaps I'm asking whether sacred imagination can be a legitimate way of boarding it.

That's what I'm exploring through Jesus TV, the Liahonas, my Jesus Pillow, the Watch Rooms, and the entire Jesus-Greg WORLD. I'm taking ordinary elements of my actual life and placing them inside a larger story about Jesus Christ.

Suppose Jesus really is present and active in my life. Suppose He is doing things I cannot yet recognize. Suppose these people, places, songs, and circumstances are part of His work. What would happen if I began living as though that possibility deserved my attention?

That's not the same as establishing that every interpretation is literally true. But it can be a meaningful religious practice. There's a difference between inventing a story to deceive myself and consciously employing sacred imagination to explore a possibility I deeply want to be true.

And I can test the fruits. Does this story help me love more? Does it make me more attentive to others? Does it move me toward the character and teachings of Christ?

Now I can almost hear Saint Greg addressing the conductor of the Otherworldly Express:

“Excuse me, sir. I don't have a ticket. I don't have an official calling. I don't have a golden plate, a seer stone, or even a decent theological résumé.

“But I have Alma 32:27. And I have a rather active imagination. And I'm sixty-two years old, which means I don't have an unlimited number of opportunities to catch this train.

“So if desiring to believe is enough to get me onto the platform, and sacred imagination is permitted in the passenger compartment, then by golly, I'M GETTING ON!

“I'll sit quietly by the window. Mostly. And if Jesus starts doing something spectacular, I intend to be watching.”

Beneath the sacred clowning is something serious: I don't want my fear of being mistaken to become the reason I never attempt the adventure.

Of course, “almost by any means necessary” is enthusiasm, not permission to abandon truthfulness or discernment. I can imaginatively join the adventure without insisting every imagined connection is an actual revelation.

I can say, “This is how I am imagining my life with Jesus. This is what I think He may be teaching me. Let's see what grows from it.”

I have only one mortal life, and if Jesus Christ is doing the greatest works in the history of the world, I do not intend to miss the train merely because I cannot yet tell the difference between a revelation, an inspired imagination, and my own magnificent capacity for making things up. I intend to seek Him, test what I think I hear, and get on with the fascination.

Maybe sacred imagination isn't merely my attempt to board the train. Maybe it is one of the ways I'm learning to recognize the train when it arrives.

Saint Greg
